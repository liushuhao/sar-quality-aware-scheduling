%  ABSTRACT (~200 words)
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
In agile SAR scheduling the choice of observation time inside a visibility
window sets the image quality, so scheduling and imaging physics act as one
decision. We formulate single-satellite Stripmap scheduling as a
three-objective problem that optimises coverage profit, geometric resolution,
and noise-equivalent sigma-zero (NESZ) radiometric quality jointly with a
non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm III (NSGA-III), and we measure where
the formulation pays off. Across 200 simulated scenarios spanning 20 to 500
targets in a two-orbit horizon, sparse request sets reward re-optimisation
over the greedy coverage baseline:
$+33.7\%$
geometric-index quality at a $14\%$ coverage cost and $+40\%$ radiometric-index
quality at a $15\%$ cost (the squint-minimising greedy heuristic reaches
$+45\%$ at a $49\%$ cost), with a controlled $+4.9\%$ third-objective margin
under a matched radiometric-aware selection rule ($+0.05\%$, $p=0.86$,
radiometric-blind).

Two measured boundaries delimit
that gain. The advantage contracts as the squint envelope narrows, from $+28\%$
to $+2.3\%$ in geometric quality when the cap tightens from $45^\circ$ to
$15^\circ$, and it vanishes beyond $N \approx 150$ (logistic transition
midpoint $N_{50} \approx 51$, CI $[37, 64]$), where the
multi-objective fronts converge to the greedy coverage anchor with quality
margins below $2\%$ under greedy hot-start initialization. The geometric and
radiometric objectives conflict most inside sparse schedules
($r \approx -0.62$, close to the $-0.60$ correlation two independent angles
would produce), and
coverage pressure weakens that conflict to between $-0.13$ and $-0.23$ at
high density,
which locates the loss of value in the schedule geometry and not in a
redundancy between objectives. A four-variant objective ablation over
$1{,}200$ further runs traces these gains to the squint channel rather than
to objective redundancy. Together the boundaries give planners two quantified operating limits:
squint-envelope contraction and a density threshold, plus an optional
operating point.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Agile Earth-observation satellites equipped with Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)
are increasingly central to time-critical monitoring missions, from disaster response
to maritime surveillance.
Their agility (the ability to slew the antenna beam away from the nadir track)
enables a single satellite to image far more targets per orbit than a fixed-pointing
platform \citep{Curlander1991}.
This flexibility, however, transforms scheduling from a simple feasibility check
into a combinatorial optimization problem: for $N$ targets each with a finite
visibility window, the scheduler must select a subset and assign observation times
that satisfy attitude transition constraints while maximizing some measure of
mission value.

Throughout this paper, the setting is a \emph{single-satellite, two-orbit-horizon} problem: one
satellite plans observations over a two-orbit scheduling horizon ($\sim 200$ min), with at most
one observation per target.

In conventional satellite scheduling, observation quality is commonly
treated as a fixed attribute of each pre-computed opportunity. Under that
treatment the scheduling problem reduces to maximizing the number or
priority-weighted sum of observed targets subject to temporal feasibility, an
assumption that underpins most existing satellite scheduling work, from
mixed-integer programming formulations to deep reinforcement learning
approaches \citep{Chen2025NeuralPriority}.

SAR imaging departs fundamentally from this assumption when the scheduler can choose the acquisition time, because image quality depends on the radar geometry at the moment of acquisition:
the ground-range resolution degrades with incidence angle ($\rho_{\text{rg}} \propto 1/\sin\theta$),
the azimuth resolution broadens with squint angle ($\rho_{\text{az}} \propto 1/\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$),
and the radiometric sensitivity (Noise-Equivalent Sigma Zero, NESZ) deteriorates
with slant range as $R^3$ \citep{Cumming2005}.
When the scheduler can choose \emph{when} within a visibility window to observe
each target (the defining feature of agile satellites), it simultaneously
chooses the imaging quality at which each target is acquired.
Despite this tight coupling between scheduling and imaging physics,
with few exceptions \citep{Kramer2025SARVis}, existing
agile SAR scheduling methods either reduce SAR to a coverage-only objective
or apply post hoc quality checks on schedules produced by
coverage-maximizing solvers.

The problem is NP-hard by reduction from the Orienteering Problem with
Time-Dependent Profits \citep{Peng2019Orienteering}, precluding
polynomial-time exact algorithms for all but the smallest instances.
The SAR literature has well-established models for NESZ and geometric resolution
as functions of observation geometry \citep{Curlander1991,Cumming2005}. We
therefore formulate SAR scheduling with separate,
physics-derived geometric and radiometric quality objectives within a
multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (MOEA) framework.

We formulate a three-objective agile SAR scheduling problem in which the
scheduler optimizes (1) total coverage profit, (2) mean geometric image quality
(ground-range and azimuth resolution), and (3) mean NESZ radiometric quality
simultaneously (denoted $f_1^*$, $f_2$, and $f_3$ below,
\S~\ref{sec:objective-functions}).
We compare five solver configurations spanning the methodological spectrum: two greedy
heuristics (the profit-only greedy baseline G-BL and one squint-minimized variant G-SM), a
single-objective genetic algorithm with greedy hot-start (GA-P-BL, a control
that returns the greedy schedule whenever it fails to improve profit, so it
is never worse than G-BL), and two NSGA-III
variants (MOEA-2 and MOEA-3) with two and three objectives.

The specific contributions of the paper are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item A three-objective formulation of single-satellite agile SAR
    scheduling that decomposes image quality into closed-form NESZ
    ($\propto R^3$) and geometric quality
    ($\sin\theta\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$, the dimensionless inverse of the ground
    pixel area since $\rho_{\text{rg}} \propto 1/\sin\theta$ and
    $\rho_{\text{az}} \propto 1/\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$) objectives inside
    NSGA-III, using mean per-task quality metrics that decouple quality from
    the number of scheduled tasks. This separates the physics from the coarse
    visibility composite of \citet{Kramer2025SARVis} and the generic
    image-quality composite of \citet{Wei2025MONP}, and enables a systematic
    quality--coverage comparison across two greedy heuristics, a
    single-objective GA control, and two NSGA-III variants on 200 scenarios in
    four scale classes;
    \item A measured operating envelope for that formulation: the size of the
    sparse-regime gain ($+33.7\%$ in $f_2$ at $N=20$), its contraction under a
    tightening squint cap (from $+28\%$ to $+2.3\%$ as the cap narrows from
    $45^\circ$ to $15^\circ$), and the density threshold beyond which the
    multi-objective variants converge to the greedy coverage anchor
    ($N_{50} \approx 51$; negligible advantage beyond $N=150$)
    (\S\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}, \S\ref{sec:envelope-sensitivity});
    \item An anchor-independent decomposition of the $f_2$--$f_3$ coupling
    that separates the trade-off carried by raw visibility geometry from the
    within-schedule residual (\S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}): measured across
    the density classes, the trade-off is mild over raw visibility geometry
    ($r \approx -0.36$ against an independent-angle null of $r \approx -0.60$),
    is restored to null strength inside sparse schedules ($-0.62$) by
    squint-aware steering, and dilutes to $-0.13$ to $-0.23$ under coverage
    pressure at high density;
    \item A controlled ablation (four objective-function variants over six
    scenario classes, 300 scenarios per variant) and initialization controls
    showing that the third objective's measurable value is concentrated in
    sparse schedules and flows through the squint channel, that solver
    outcomes become invariant to the choice of physical objectives at high
    target density (where the objective is retained for quality-assessment
    completeness rather than incremental gain), and that coverage convergence
    is conditional on greedy-baseline (G-BL) hot-start initialization
    (\S\ref{sec:ablation}, \S\ref{sec:robustness-calibration}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
We examine three lines of research that our study connects:
satellite scheduling (2.1), SAR physical quality modeling (2.2),
and multi-objective evolutionary optimization (2.3).
\subsection{Satellite Scheduling}
Satellite scheduling is surveyed in depth by \citet{Ferrari2025} (over 200 publications
across exact, heuristic, metaheuristic, and learning methods). Mixed-integer linear
programming is exact on small instances but does not scale, motivating genetic
algorithms, simulated annealing, tabu search~\citep{Wang2019MILP,Chen2019MILPMulti},
a Lagrangian-relaxation heuristic for multi-satellite planning~\citep{Wang2026Lagrangian},
and constraint programming for the super-agile problem~\citep{Caleiras2026SuperAgile};
\citet{Lemaitre2002MultiSat} and \citet{Bianchessi2007} established the agile problem's
time-dependent transition structure, and recent work packs more observations per orbit
via free start times within visibility windows~\citep{Peng2019Orienteering,Chen2024LearnConstruct}.
A fast-growing parallel line is learning-based: \citet{Liu2024TwoStageDRL} and
\citet{Zhou2025TwoPhase} apply deep reinforcement learning to agile optical satellite
scheduling, \citet{Jacquet2024GNN} combine graph networks with Monte Carlo tree search, and
\citet{Herrmann2023} apply RL on-board without imaging quality; for
high-density AEOS, \citet{Wu2026WhaleHighDensity} report convergence challenges echoing
the density-driven tightening we analyze in \S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}.
Most prior work treats imaging quality as fixed at window generation, whereas SAR quality
varies continuously with acquisition geometry and is directly optimizable inside the
scheduling window. The SAR-specific branch applies multi-objective evolutionary algorithms
(MOEAs) to this gap, but with quality as a scalar or proxy: \citet{Chang2022Gaofen} pair
large-neighborhood search with NSGA-II/III over a three-objective operational model
(ground-target loss, invalid observations, sensor activations; the closest prior work
to ours, but without imaging quality); \citet{Wei2025MONP} use a neural-policy MOEA over a
composite score; \citet{Kramer2025SARVis} embed a quality proxy for constellations;
\citet{Yao2024Bilevel} separate allocation from detailed planning;
\citet{ChangZhou2025Memetic} attach an attitude-angle proxy;
\citet{Wu2019NSGA3Formation} use NSGA-III with coverage and timeliness objectives.
The common limitation of this prior work is that SAR physical quality
metrics such as noise-equivalent sigma zero (NESZ), azimuth resolution, and
ground-range resolution are either absent or approximated by coarse
proxies (e.g., penalizing extreme off-nadir angles). To our knowledge,
no existing SAR scheduling method treats continuous, physics-derived
quality functions as decomposed optimization objectives in their own
right: the closest approaches embed aggregated quality
proxies rather than separate geometric and radiometric objectives.
\subsection{SAR Physical Quality Modeling}
The SAR remote sensing literature provides well-established analytical
models linking observation geometry to image quality.
\citet{Curlander1991}, \citet{Cumming2005}, and \citet{Moreira2026} give detailed treatments
of SAR physics, including the two quality dimensions relevant to scheduling:
geometric resolution and radiometric sensitivity.
Geometric resolution decomposes into ground-range resolution
$\rho_{\text{rg}} = c / (2B \sin\theta)$, where $c$ is the speed of light,
$B$ the radar bandwidth, and $\theta$ the incidence angle, and azimuth
resolution $\rho_{\text{az}} = D / (2 \cos\psi_{\text{sq}})$, where $D$
is the antenna length and $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ the squint angle. In
operational SAR, amplitude weighting widens $\rho_{\text{az}}$ by
10--20\%~\citep{Curlander1991}, but since this is platform-constant, the
unweighted form preserves the relative ordering for optimization.
The two resolution components respond oppositely to departure from the
zero-Doppler geometry ($\psi_{\text{sq}} = 0$, $\theta$ minimal):
$\rho_{\text{az}}$ worsens as $|\psi_{\text{sq}}|$ grows, whereas
$\rho_{\text{rg}}$ is coarsest at the minimal incidence of closest approach
and improves as $\theta$ rises away from it, the origin of the $\theta$-axis
trade-off formalized in \S~\ref{sec:objective-functions}. Radiometric sensitivity,
measured by the Noise-Equivalent Sigma Zero (NESZ), deteriorates with the
cube of slant range: $\text{NESZ} \propto R^3$ for Stripmap SAR after azimuth compression
gain~\citep{Curlander1991,Moreira2026}. The specific range decomposition and
ordering-index approximation used in this work are derived in
\S~\ref{sec:objective-functions}.
Our study
connects these two domains by embedding continuous quality functions
directly into the optimization objective space.
\subsection{Multi-Objective Evolutionary Optimization}
Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms are a standard toolkit
for problems with competing objectives. The NSGA-III algorithm
\citep{Deb2014} extends the NSGA-II framework \citep{Deb2002NSGA2} to many-objective problems
through reference-point-based diversity preservation.
The hypervolume (HV) indicator \citep{Zitzler1999HV} is a standard
Pareto-front comparison tool; its interpretational caveats for mixing
single- and multi-objective solvers are handled in
\S~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics} and \S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}.
For this study's use, three caveats matter. First, with origin-anchored reference boxes
HV is insensitive to cardinality---dominated points add no volume, so one extreme point
can out-volume an entire front. Second, it is blind to decision-space diversity, making
front-ranking claims fragile when single-point greedy solvers and multi-point NSGA-III
fronts are mixed. Third, these properties rule out HV as a standalone quality judgment,
so we pair it with per-objective effect sizes and report it as a secondary diagnostic
with explicit point-count controls.
Our NSGA-III formulation makes these trade-offs explicit by placing
coverage profit, geometric quality, and radiometric quality in a unified
multi-objective framework.

\section{Problem Formulation}
% ── 3.1 System Model ──
\subsection{System Model}\label{sec:system-model}
% Satellite orbit, task set, visibility windows
% Key parameters: H, ω_max, τ_settle
% Decision variables: x_i (selection), t_i (start time)
We consider a single agile SAR satellite in a circular orbit at altitude $H$,
operating in Stripmap mode, which provides the widest continuous swath with
uniform quality and analytically tractable NESZ and resolution expressions
\citep{Curlander1991}.
A problem instance $\mathcal{I} = (\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{O})$ consists of
a set of $N$ ground targets $\mathcal{T}$, a satellite platform $\mathcal{P}$,
and a SAR imaging model $\mathcal{O}$.
\paragraph{Task Set.}
Each task $i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$ is specified by its geographic coordinates
$\mathbf{r}_i = (\phi_i, \lambda_i)$, a priority weight $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^+$, a visibility window
$\mathcal{W}_i = [a_i, b_i]$ during which the satellite can observe the target,
and a fixed observation duration $d_i$.
\paragraph{Satellite Platform.}
The satellite is characterized by its maximum slew rate $\omega_{\max}$ (a
conservative single-axis bound on three-axis attitude maneuvers), settling
time $\tau_{\text{settle}}$ after each maneuver, per-orbit energy budget
$E_{\max}$ and memory budget $M_{\max}$, and per-observation energy $e_i$
and memory $m_i$ consumption.
\paragraph{SAR Observables.}
Given an observation time $t_i$, the satellite-to-target line-of-sight (LOS)
vector $\mathbf{l}(t_i)$ in the Local-Vertical Local-Horizontal (LVLH) frame
is fully determined by the satellite's orbital state.
From $\mathbf{l}(t_i)$ we derive four geometric quantities deterministically:
slant range $R = |\mathbf{l}|$, off-nadir angle $\xi = \arctan2(\sqrt{l_x^2+l_y^2},\, l_z)$,
incidence angle $\theta$ (via the Earth-curvature-corrected mapping
$\theta = \arcsin\big(\frac{R_e+H}{R_e}\sin\xi\big)$ where $R_e$ is Earth's mean radius), and
squint angle $\psi_{\text{sq}} = \arcsin(|l_x| / R)$ (LVLH $x$-axis
along-track, $z$-axis nadir-pointing), defined as the
angular deviation of the LOS vector from the zero-Doppler plane
\citep{Curlander1991}.
For the objectives we also use the elevation-plane incidence angle
$\theta_{\text{elev}}$, defined through the identity
$\cos\theta_{\text{elev}} = \cos\xi / \cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$
(i.e., the incidence angle of the LOS projection onto the cross-track plane);
this isolates the cross-track ground-range geometry from the along-track
squint contribution. $\theta_{\text{elev}}$ is the satellite-centered
elevation-plane angle; it differs from the target-side incidence angle
defined above by $2.0^\circ$ at an $18^\circ$ off-nadir angle and $7.2^\circ$ at
$47^\circ$ in the zero-squint plane due to Earth curvature at
these orbital altitudes, but it preserves the cross-track ordering of
candidate views, so using it in the geometric-quality objective of
\S~\ref{sec:objective-functions} is ordering-consistent.
A critical property is that \emph{all} quality-relevant geometry
is a deterministic function of the single decision $t_i$.
\paragraph{Decision Variables.}
A schedule consists of a task selection vector $\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$,
an ordered sequence $S = (s_1,\ldots,s_m)$ of the $m$ selected tasks, and
a vector of observation start times $\mathbf{t} = (t_{s_1},\ldots,t_{s_m})$.
All geometric parameters ($\theta_i$, $\psi_{\text{sq},i}$, $R_i$) are
\emph{derived} from $t_i$ rather than independent decision variables.
% ── 3.2 Objective Functions ──
\subsection{Objective Functions}\label{sec:objective-functions}
We formulate a three-objective optimization problem that captures the
tension between scheduling throughput and SAR-specific imaging quality.
All quality objectives use the \emph{mean} over scheduled tasks, making
them independent of the number of scheduled tasks $m = |\mathcal{S}|$
and thereby producing a genuine ``quantity versus quality'' Pareto trade-off.
\paragraph{Objective 1: Coverage Profit.}
The normalized coverage profit (Eq.~\ref{eq:f1}) is defined relative to the greedy baseline
\begin{equation}
f_1^* = \frac{1}{f_1^{\text{G-BL}}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} p_i
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
where $f_1^{\text{G-BL}}$ is the profit achieved by the profit-only greedy
scheduler (G-BL, \S~\ref{sec:gbl}) on the same scenario; normalization by
G-BL rather than GA-P-BL is conservative for the MOEA coverage deficit
(\S~\ref{sec:overall-performance}).
\paragraph{Objective 2: Geometric Image Quality.}
The ground pixel area of a SAR image is the product of ground-range resolution
$\rho_{\text{rg}} = c / (2B \sin\theta)$ and azimuth resolution
$\rho_{\text{az}} = D / (2 \cos\psi_{\text{sq}})$.
We define a dimensionless geometric quality index:
\begin{equation}
f_2 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} \sin\theta_{\text{elev},i} \cdot \cos\psi_{\text{sq},i}
\label{eq:f2}
\end{equation}
Higher $f_2$ indicates a smaller per-task ground-pixel-area factor (better geometric resolution).
The quality indices are unweighted means over the scheduled set, orthogonal to
the priority weights used in $f_1$: they reflect the average imaging geometry
of the selected tasks rather than biasing quality toward high-priority targets.
\paragraph{Objective 3: NESZ Radiometric Quality.}
Noise-Equivalent Sigma Zero (NESZ) measures the minimum detectable backscatter.
For Stripmap SAR, NESZ degrades with the cube of slant range,
$\text{NESZ} \propto R^3$, after accounting for azimuth compression gain \citep{Curlander1991}.
Using the full off-nadir angle $\xi$ for the $R^3$ factor (which already
contains the squint contribution through $\cos\xi = \cos\theta_{\text{elev}}\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$,
so no separate squint factor is needed), we define the radiometric quality index as:
\begin{equation}
f_3 = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} \cos^3\xi_i
\label{eq:f3}
\end{equation}
Higher $f_3$ indicates a lower per-task NESZ factor (better radiometric sensitivity).

\paragraph{Modeling choices behind Eq.~\ref{eq:f3}.}
Equation~\ref{eq:f3} rests on a 3D geometric approximation that writes the slant range as an
elevation-plane range at zero squint over $\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$, yielding $\text{NESZ} \propto
1/(\cos^3\theta_{\text{elev}} \cdot \cos^3\psi_{\text{sq}})$; $f_3$ serves as a relative ordering index.

\paragraph{Modeling approximation and error.}
The plane-Earth model omits Earth curvature and the antenna elevation-pattern roll-off
($4\%$/$13\%$ slant-range/$R^3$ underestimation at the $47^\circ$ edge) and suppresses the
ground-reference $\sin\theta$ factor; the suppression itself is ordering-preserving by
construction (\S\ref{sec:system-model}). The comparisons in \S\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}
and below depend only on the induced ordering---strongest within windows (mean incidence
varies by about $1.5^\circ$ in S1; \S\ref{sec:ablation}), while across $18^\circ$--$47^\circ$
the suppressed factor varies by roughly $2.4\times$; $f_3$ is a geometry proxy, not an
absolute NESZ ranking.

\paragraph{Squint magnitude and $R^3$ dominance.}
In the produced schedules the per-task squint is moderate (mean $|\psi_{\text{sq}}|$ of $16$--$24^\circ$), so
the $\cos^3\psi_{\text{sq}}$ factor contributes $10$--$25\%$ of the $f_3$ range; $\text{NESZ} \propto R^3$ captures
the dominant geometric dependence, and platform-constant terms do not affect the ordering.
\paragraph{Physical Trade-Off.}
The two quality objectives partition SAR imaging performance by mechanism: $f_2$ spatial
resolution (geometric), $f_3$ signal-to-noise ratio (radiometric). Their optima conflict:
$f_3$ peaks at the zero-Doppler point ($\psi_{\text{sq}} = 0$) whereas $f_2$ benefits from
moderately higher incidence---radiometric cleanliness against ground pixel area. The
squint angle enters both objectives (Eqs.~\ref{eq:f2}--\ref{eq:f3}) through the same
$\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$ factor, so squint is an alignment rather than a trade-off axis.
Since $\theta$ and $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ derive from the same LOS vector
(\S\ref{sec:system-model}), the per-task $f_2$ and $f_3$ contributions share geometric
structure, quantified in \S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}.
% ── 3.3 Constraints ──
\subsection{Constraints}\label{sec:constraints}
Observation-window membership is treated as the domain of each start-time
variable rather than as a separate numbered constraint:
\begin{equation}
t_i \in [a_i, b_i - d_i] \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{S}.
\label{eq:observation-domain}
\end{equation}
The remaining four constraints couple task selection, acquisition geometry,
temporal feasibility, and resource use. The incidence-angle and squint-angle
constraints (C1) are enforced during visibility-window generation; C2--C4 are checked
inline by every solver.

\paragraph{C1: Incidence and Squint Angle Constraints.}
Each selected acquisition must satisfy the platform incidence-angle range,
$\theta^{\min} \leq |\theta_i| \leq \theta^{\max}$, and the squint-angle limit
$|\psi_{\text{sq},i}| \leq \psi_{\text{sq}}^{\max}$, where $\psi_{\text{sq}}^{\max}$
is the satellite's maximum achievable squint; we adopt $|\psi_{\text{sq}}| \leq 45^\circ$
as a controlled experimental agility envelope. This bound is an experimental
setting, not a claim about any specific platform's demonstrated operating
mode or capability.
Within C1-feasible visibility geometry the usable envelope is effectively
narrower: window generation filters at the 10\,s sampling grid, and the
$\theta$--$\psi_{\text{sq}}$ coupling of the LOS vector (the window-level
$|\psi_{\text{sq}}|$ 95th percentile is $37^\circ$ and the maximum $45^\circ$)
confines feasible acquisitions; the selected
schedules concentrate at mean $|\psi_{\text{sq}}|$ of $16$--$24^\circ$
(\S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}).
Both are observation-geometry limits applied before scheduling.
Modeling scope and its limits are collected in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{C2: Attitude Maneuver and Non-Overlap.}
Between consecutive observations, the satellite must rotate through the
angular separation of their LOS vectors. Let $\mathbf{l}_k$ and
$\mathbf{l}_{k+1}$ be the LVLH-frame LOS vectors at $t_{s_k}$ and
$t_{s_{k+1}}$. The angular gap
$\Delta\eta_k = \arccos(\mathbf{l}_k\cdot\mathbf{l}_{k+1}/(|\mathbf{l}_k||\mathbf{l}_{k+1}|))$
must be traversed within the available inter-observation time:
\begin{equation}
t_{s_{k+1}} \geq t_{s_k} + d_{s_k} + \frac{\Delta\eta_k}{\omega_{\max}} + \tau_{\text{settle}} \quad \forall k \in \{1,\ldots,m-1\}.
\label{eq:c2}
\end{equation}
This inequality also enforces non-overlapping task-execution intervals. It is
the core coupling in our formulation: changing $t_{s_k}$ shifts
$\mathbf{l}_k$, affecting both imaging quality (via $\theta_k$ and
$\psi_{\text{sq},k}$) and transition feasibility (via $\Delta\eta_k$).

The tightness of C2 scales with task density: as $N$ grows, the fraction
of task pairs whose required angular transition
$\Delta\eta / \omega_{\max}$ exceeds the available inter-observation
time increases, tightening the constraint; C2 is the dominant active
constraint in most dense-regime scenarios, except where the C3--C4
resource caps bind (see below).

\paragraph{C3--C4: Energy and Memory Budgets.}
$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} e_i \leq E_{\max}$ and
$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} m_i \leq M_{\max}$, where $E_{\max}=10^7$ and
$M_{\max}=10^{11}$ (arbitrary units) and typical per-task consumptions are
$e_i = 5\times10^4$ and $m_i = 5\times10^8$. Both caps therefore translate
into the same ceiling of 200 concurrently scheduled tasks---inactive in
sparse regimes, but binding in a subset of dense scenarios whose
consequences for the quality measurements are quantified in
\S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}.

\section{Methodology}
% ── 4.1 Solver Overview ──
\subsection{Solver Overview}
Table~\ref{tab:solvers} summarizes the five configurations compared in this study.
We describe the solvers in order of increasing sophistication: from greedy heuristics
(G-BL, G-SM) through single-objective metaheuristic (GA-P-BL), to multi-objective

paper's research question. A comparison across algorithmic families
(e.g., NSGA-III vs.\ MOEA/D (decomposition-based) vs.\ SMS-EMOA (hypervolume-based)) is left to future work
(\S~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\subsection{G-BL: Greedy Baseline}
\label{sec:gbl}
G-BL represents the conventional scheduling approach:
it makes no use of SAR imaging physics when ordering or selecting tasks.
Tasks are sorted by earliest start time $t_{\text{start}}$ and greedily inserted
into the schedule at their earliest feasible time.
This earliest-time placement fixes each task's observation geometry implicitly
through the window's incidence--squint profile rather than through any quality
criterion.
If the attitude transition constraint (C2) is violated for a candidate task,
its start time is delayed within the window until the constraint is satisfied;
if no feasible delay exists, the task is dropped.
Since G-BL selects observation times solely for feasibility, its $f_2$ and
$f_3$ values are incidental by-products of the window geometry.
We compute these post hoc on the final schedule to serve as the baseline
against which physics-aware solvers are compared.
\subsection{G-SM: Greedy Squint-Minimized}
G-SM shares G-BL's greedy structure (sort by earliest start time) but modifies the
observation time selection: within each visibility window, a task is
placed at the discretized time step that minimizes squint angle
$\psi_{\text{sq}} = \arcsin(|l_x| / R)$.
G-SM minimizes squint angle as a computationally cheap proxy for
maximizing $f_3$ (NESZ radiometric quality); the correlation between
squint minimization and $f_3$ improvement is confirmed by the large
effect size (matched-pairs sign statistic $\delta_{\text{pairs}} = +0.99$, \S~\ref{sec:squint-special}).
This strategy serves as a physics-aware greedy baseline: it answers the question
``what quality improvement can a simple squint-minimization heuristic
achieve, without any multi-objective search?''
The squint-minimizing time selection incurs a profit penalty because
the optimal-squint time may lie later in the window, reducing the
remaining time for subsequent tasks (constraint C2).
As with G-BL, $f_2$ and $f_3$ are post hoc outputs.
\subsection{GA-P-BL: Single-Objective GA with Hot-Start}
\label{sec:ga-p-bl}
GA-P-BL tests whether a genetic algorithm optimizing only $f_1$ can
outperform greedy scheduling by exploiting temporal flexibility.
Each chromosome encodes a vector of normalized start times
$\boldsymbol{\tau} \in [0,1]^N$, where task $i$'s actual start time is
$t_i = t_i^{\text{earliest}} + \tau_i \cdot (t_i^{\text{latest}} - d_i - t_i^{\text{earliest}})$,
alongside a binary selection vector $\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$; both are
decision variables (a $2N$-dimensional chromosome).
The population of 100 individuals is initialized from G-BL's solution
with Gaussian noise (std = 0.5) added to the full $2N$-dimensional chromosome.
The GA runs for 200 generations with simulated binary crossover
($p_c = 0.9$, $\eta_c = 20$) and polynomial mutation
($p_m = 0.1$, $\eta_m = 20$).
$f_2$ and $f_3$ are computed post hoc on the best-found schedule.
After optimization, a never-worse-than-baseline guard compares the best GA
schedule with G-BL: if the GA's $f_1$ falls short, the greedy schedule is
returned instead, guaranteeing $f_1^{\text{GA-P-BL}} \ge f_1^{\text{G-BL}}$ by
construction. In practice this guard makes GA-P-BL output numerically
identical to G-BL in 199 of 200 scenarios (the single exception improves
profit by $13.5\%$; mean $f_2$ and $f_3$ are within $0.001$ of G-BL at
every scale)
(\S\ref{sec:overall-performance}); GA-P-BL therefore acts as a control that
confirms the greedy profit baseline rather than as an independent solver.
\subsection{MOEA-2 and MOEA-3: Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms}
\label{sec:moea-description}
MOEA-2 and MOEA-3 extend the GA-P-BL chromosome encoding to
multi-objective optimization via the NSGA-III framework
\citep{Deb2014}.
Both use a population of 100 individuals over 200 generations with
the same genetic operators as GA-P-BL.
Reference points are generated via the Das--Dennis systematic
approach \citep{DasDennis1998}, producing $H = \binom{n_{\text{obj}} + p - 1}{p}$ structured
reference directions, where $n_{\text{obj}}$ is the number of objectives
and $p = 12$ the divisions per objective dimension
($H = 13$ for MOEA-2, $H = 91$ for MOEA-3).
The entire population is hot-started from the G-BL solution perturbed
by Gaussian noise (std = 0.5), and an at-least-one-task constraint
prevents empty solutions from dominating the Pareto front. As every
solver is hot-started from the non-empty G-BL solution
(\S~\ref{sec:gbl}), this constraint is inactive in the reported
experiments.
MOEA-2 optimizes two objectives: $f_1^*$ (normalized profit) and
$f_2$ (mean geometric quality, Eq.~\ref{eq:f2}).
MOEA-3 adds $f_3$ (mean NESZ radiometric quality, Eq.~\ref{eq:f3})
as a third objective.
The comparison between MOEA-2 and MOEA-3 directly tests whether
adding the NESZ objective ($f_3$) produces Pareto-front differentiation
beyond what $f_2$ alone captures, or whether the $\theta$/$\psi_{\text{sq}}$
geometric coupling renders the third objective redundant in the
two-orbit-horizon, single-satellite setting.

All scenarios share a common satellite platform abstracted as a generic
C-band Stripmap SAR with bilateral-looking capability. The platform follows
a circular sun-synchronous orbit at 693 km altitude
(inclination \mbox{$\approx 97.8^\circ$}, LTAN 06:00, dawn--dusk) and carries
a C-band Stripmap instrument with antenna length $D = 12.3$ m and bandwidth
$B = 56.5$ MHz. It accesses both left- and right-side incidence angles in
the range $[18^\circ, 47^\circ]$ (signed convention:
$\theta \in [-47^\circ, -18^\circ] \cup [18^\circ, 47^\circ]$). This generic
bilateral configuration is selected to exercise the full incidence--squint
trade-off space.

Targets are distributed along the satellite ground track within the
accessible observation band (cross-track half-width roughly $\pm 608$ km,
a ground central angle of about $\pm 5.5^\circ$, corresponding to the
$47^\circ$ incidence limit). Within each class, five scenario variants
span the target distribution (uniform, clustered, and mixed) and geometry:
variants A--C modulate only the target distribution, while variants D and E
additionally modulate the observation geometry, with a class-dependent role:
S1-D and S3-D use a single orbital revolution (yielding fewer and narrower
windows), S2-E places targets at high latitudes ($\pm 40$--$70^\circ$), and
S4-D narrows the incidence ceiling from $47^\circ$ to $25^\circ$. The 50 scenarios per class consist of these 5 variants
$\times$ 10 random seeds each; the density trend reported in
\S\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity} is robust to these within-class modulations
(e.g., the sparse-regime coverage deficit appears across all S1 variants, and
the densest S4 class remains at or near full coverage even for the variants
with fewer or tighter windows). The trend also holds with variant A alone (distribution-only modulation,
identical geometry definition across classes): the MOEA-2 $f_2$
gain over G-BL is $+37.2\%$ (S1), $+3.5\%$ (S2), $+0.4\%$ (S3), and $+0.3\%$
(S4), reproducing the saturation on the pure density axis; the within-schedule
$f_2$--$f_3$ correlation follows the same trend on this axis (pooled per-task
$r$ over the 10 variant-A schedules per class: $-0.80$, $-0.23$, $-0.15$,
$-0.31$ from S1 to S4, mirroring the dilution trend of
\S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling} on this smaller variant-A-only subset). The four density
classes (S1--S4) span sparse reconnaissance ($N=20$) to dense monitoring
($N=500$). Other intermediate densities ($N=50, 400$; classes S5--S6 in the
scenario library) are omitted to limit computational cost. To directly
constrain the transition midpoint, two additional intermediate classes
(S7 at $N=150$ and S8 at $N=200$, continuing the numbering, 50 scenarios
each, generated under the same generic C-band bilateral configuration) were run for
MOEA-2 and the two greedy baselines (G-BL, G-SM) and are used solely in the
scale-sensitivity analysis in \S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}; they do not enter
the five-solver pooled comparisons.

Each task is assigned a priority weight $p_i$ drawn uniformly from the integer set $\{1, 2, \dots, 10\}$, and a fixed observation
duration $d_i = 30$\,s. Visibility windows are computed via orbit propagation over two orbital periods
($\sim$200 min), yielding 1--2 windows per target on average, each
typically spanning about a minute (median 1.0--1.2\,min across the density
classes). Candidate observation times are sampled at a 10\,s step
within each window, and all reported numbers correspond to a fixed scenario
manifest (no manifest revision postdates the results). Windows are filtered by incidence-angle,
squint, and minimum-elevation constraints at generation time; the quality
indices are then evaluated at each scheduled acquisition time, and C1
feasibility is additionally verified over the full 30\,s observation
interval (a 0.5\,s-resolution scan of all 76{,}190 windows across the four
density classes reports zero violations; the median change of squint over
any 30\,s within-window sub-interval is $11^\circ$, 95th percentile
$15^\circ$).
% ── 5.2 Solver Configuration ──
\subsection{Solver Configuration}
All five configurations run on a single CPU core to ensure a uniform computational environment. For orientation, single-core wall-clock per scenario is milliseconds for the greedy heuristics and a median of about 22\,s (S1), 66\,s (S2), 220\,s (S3), and 260\,s (S4) for the NSGA-III variants; the study's claims concern solution quality rather than computational efficiency, so these are order-of-magnitude indications rather than a benchmark.
G-BL and G-SM are deterministic greedy heuristics with no tunable
hyperparameters. GA-P-BL and the MOEA variants use the population size,
generation budget, and genetic operators described in \S\ref{sec:ga-p-bl}
and \S\ref{sec:moea-description}, with the entire MOEA population
hot-started from the G-BL solution. The NSGA-III selection operator follows
the reference-point-based selection of \citet{Deb2014}.
% ── 5.3 Evaluation Metrics ──
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:evaluation-metrics}
We treat per-objective comparisons paired with Cliff's $\delta$ effect sizes
as the primary quality indicator, because single-point greedy solvers and
multi-point NSGA-III fronts contribute different numbers of points to the
objective space. Hypervolume (HV) is reported as a secondary
diagnostic only: with origin-anchored reference boxes it is insensitive to
cardinality (dominated points add no volume), but it is also blind to
decision-space diversity --- as shown in \S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling},
its ranking inverts if the single-point G-SM is included (its HV of $0.2341$
exceeds MOEA-3's $0.0893$), so HV is not interpreted as a standalone quality
judgment. Statistical significance is assessed via the Friedman test for
overall differences across the five configurations $\times$ 200 scenarios, followed by
pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons with Bonferroni correction
($\alpha = 0.05 / 10 = 0.005$); because G-BL and GA-P-BL coincide in all but
one scenario (\S\ref{sec:ga-p-bl}), the Friedman statistic is conservative
toward fewer distinct rankings and is
driven largely by within-scenario dispersion of the two multi-point NSGA-III
solvers. Per-objective and ablation comparisons are
reported with raw $p$-values alongside the Bonferroni threshold. Effect
sizes are measured by Cliff's $\delta$, classified following
\citet{Romano2006} ($|\delta| < 0.147$ negligible,
$< 0.33$ small, $< 0.474$ medium, $\geq 0.474$ large).
For paired within-scenario solver comparisons we report the matched-pairs
sign statistic $\delta_{\text{pairs}} = (W - L)/n$, the matched-pairs
restriction of Cliff's $\delta$ (counting only within-scenario pairs); the
conventional unpaired form
$\delta = \bigl(\#\{x_i>y_j\}-\#\{x_i<y_j\}\bigr)/(nm)$
gives smaller magnitudes for the same comparisons (e.g.\ $+0.57$ versus
$+0.88$ for the sparse MOEA-3/MOEA-2 $f_3$ comparison), and Romano's
thresholds are applied to that conventional form wherever both are quoted.
Hypervolume is computed with reference point $(0, 0, 0)$ in the
min--max-normalized objective space, where the six min/max bounds
($f_1$: $0.06$--$1.16$; $f_2$: $0.11$--$0.56$; $f_3$: $0.45$--$0.87$) are
pooled across all solvers, scenarios, and every persisted non-dominated
frontier point (not only knee solutions); per-scenario HV is then averaged
within and across classes.
This shared normalization framework makes cross-solver HV values directly
comparable as relative rankings: every solver's persisted frontier
points, including the two-objective MOEA-2 fronts and the single-point
greedy solutions, carry all three coordinates ($f_3$ is evaluated post hoc
for solvers that do not optimize it), so each HV is computed in the same
normalized three-objective space, subject to the HV caveat stated
above and detailed in \S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}.

For each MOEA scenario, a single representative solution is selected from the
feasible non-dominated front as the knee point: the objectives are min--max
normalized across that scenario's front, and the point maximizing the normalized
sum ($f_1+f_2$ for MOEA-2, $f_1+f_2+f_3$ for MOEA-3) is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles} and the per-objective means. The full front is
retained for HV and for the Pareto compression analysis
(\S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}). We verified the selector sensitivity
directly: at $N\geq100$ the knee and best-$f_1$ points give class-mean $f_2$
and $f_3$ within $0.005$, because the dense-regime fronts are compressed
(S4 spans only about $0.014$ in $f_2$); at sparse S1 the knee and best-$f_1$
means differ by about $0.04$--$0.06$ in $f_2$ and $f_3$, which is expected and
intentional: Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles} reports the balancing knee
rather than either single-objective extreme. The S1 fronts bracket the knee
($f_2 = 0.376$, $f_3 = 0.698$, $f_1^* = 0.85$): the best-$f_2$ point reaches
$f_2 = 0.449$ at $f_1^* = 0.58$, and the best-$f_3$ point reaches
$f_3 = 0.738$ at $f_1^* = 0.68$, so the headline figures are conservative
compromise values rather than achievable optima. Quality gains are also
class-mean figures: because the objectives are unweighted per-task means,
a positive class-mean $f_2$/$f_3$ gain does not selectively favor
high-priority targets, and per-scenario spread persists even at dense
classes (S4 MOEA-3 $f_2$ 10th percentile $0.291$ vs.\ G-BL mean $0.336$).

\subsection{Experiment Design Overview}
\label{sec:experiment-design}

This study comprises the following classes of experiments.
The first is the main experiment, running all five configurations (G-BL, G-SM,
the GA-P-BL control, MOEA-2, MOEA-3)
on 50 scenarios per density class (S1--S4), totaling 200 scenarios.
Each scenario is solved once per stochastic configuration (the scenario seed
governs instance generation; the NSGA-III sampling seed is fixed at the
library default while operator-level random draws are not pinned), so
within-instance search stochasticity is not replicated; the released
processed results pin the exact front realizations analyzed.
A replicated-run check quantifies the cost of this design: each NSGA-III
solver is rerun five times on the same instance for ten variant-A scenarios
per class (400 solves total). At the class-mean level, the quality metrics
($f_2$, $f_3$) shift by less than $0.01$ in every class, and $f_1^*$ by less
than $0.04$ in the worst sparse-class pair --- well below the
solver-vs-baseline gaps the headline conclusions rest on (e.g.\ $0.10$ in
$f_2$ and $0.20$ in $f_3$ at S1). At the scenario level, $91\%$ of the main-run knee
values fall inside the five-repeat envelope of the same instance; the
largest deviations occur in sparse scenarios, where small schedules can
settle into distinct local optima. Pinning the remaining process-level
randomness is left as a reproducibility recommendation. In addition, the
agility-envelope sensitivity is probed directly on the sparse $N=20$
subset under tightened squint caps
($25^\circ$, $15^\circ$; \S\ref{sec:envelope-sensitivity}).
The second is the ablation experiment, which successively removes the squint component
(variant B), the incidence-angle component (variant C), and all physical objectives
(variant D) from the MOEA-3 framework, running each variant on the same
six scenario classes (300 scenarios; \S\ref{sec:ablation}).
The third comprises robustness control experiments (hot-start dependence,
perturbation-sensitivity sweep, random-search calibration, and search-budget control),
which verify that the main and ablation findings are not artifacts of initialization
or parameter choices (\S\ref{sec:robustness-calibration}).

the anchor carries bounded headroom.

The Friedman test on pooled HV is reported as a descriptive omnibus only:
it confirms overall differences among the five
solvers across all 200 scenarios ($\chi^2=669.02$, $p<10^{-140}$; conservative toward fewer
distinct rankings, \S~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}), and the inferential
weight rests on the per-objective pairwise analyses of
Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles}. G-BL, GA-P-BL, and G-SM each contribute a single point
per scenario, so the pooled statistic is driven largely by the within-scenario
HV dispersion of the two multi-point NSGA-III solvers rather than by
baseline-vs-baseline differences. Pairwise Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests use the Bonferroni threshold $\alpha=0.005$
($\alpha=0.05/10$, the family of ten pairwise solver comparisons; the
four-variant ablation of \S~\ref{sec:ablation} applies $\alpha=0.05/12$
within each reported metric, each family of 12 being the three non-physics
variants by four density classes against A; the S7--S8 ablation comparisons
are reported as exploratory and lie outside these families); pairs
with zero within-pair difference are dropped from the Wilcoxon signed-rank
statistic and reported separately; interpretation
relies on Cliff's $\delta$ given the large sample size.
MOEA-2 and MOEA-3 differ on pooled HV with $\delta = -0.56$
($p < 10^{-5}$), but this contrast blends the sparse-regime divergence with
the dense-regime convergence and is inflated by the HV point-count
asymmetry (\S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}); contrasts involving
G-SM are large because it occupies an extreme radiometric-quality regime.

The following subsections first map the density landscape---how the
multi-objective advantage contracts with target density
(\S\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity})---then isolate the squint-aware special
regime (\S\ref{sec:squint-special}), decompose the underlying mechanism
(\S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}), test whether the measured gains survive
ablation and the initialization and budget controls
(\S\ref{sec:ablation}--\S\ref{sec:robustness-calibration}),
probe the agility-envelope sensitivity (\S\ref{sec:envelope-sensitivity}),
and finally quantify the coverage-stratified frontier control against the
cheapest placement-only alternative (\S\ref{sec:frontier-control}).

% ── 6.2 Scale Sensitivity (Fig 3) ──
\subsection{Scale Sensitivity}
\label{sec:scale-sensitivity}
The gap between MOEA-2 and G-BL varies systematically with scenario density
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}, Table~\ref{tab:scale-sensitivity}).
MOEA-2's coverage deficit relative to G-BL is confined to sparse densities:
from $f_1^* = 0.86 \pm 0.13$ at S1 ($N = 20$) it rises to
$0.98 \pm 0.03$ at S2 ($N = 100$), and reaches $0.996$--$0.999$ at
$N \geq 150$ (S7--S8, S3), remaining within $1\%$ of G-BL at S4 ($0.996$).
The $f_2$ improvement over G-BL follows the same contraction, falling from $+33.7\%$ (S1)
to $+3.6\%$ (S2) and to $+1.3\%$ or less at $N \geq 150$.
Scenario-level frequency tells the same story: at S1, $80\%$ of scenarios
show a measurable coverage deficit ($f_1^* < 0.95$, i.e., a deficit
exceeding 5\%), at S2 $10\%$, and $0\%$ at $N \geq 150$.
The coverage cost of multi-objective optimization is therefore a sparse-density phenomenon:
between $N = 20$ and $N = 100$ the solver transitions from actively trading off
coverage for quality to operating at the greedy baseline, and beyond
$N = 150$ the deficit is effectively zero.

Fitting a logistic regression to the proportion of scenarios with

($N_{50} \in [37, 64]$). $N_{50}$ should therefore be read as a coarse indicator that the
transition is confined to $N \in [20, 100]$, not as a precise threshold.
Beyond $N \approx 150$, no scenario shows a
coverage deficit exceeding $5\%$. Refitting the same logistic on the

sparse-regime phenomenon, not an artefact of the bound/unbound mix.

The ceiling underlying this split is the C3--C4 resource cap of
\S\ref{sec:constraints}: it is inactive in sparse regimes but binds in a
subset of dense scenarios---20 of 50 G-BL schedules at S3 and 27 of 50 at
S4 select exactly 200 tasks, so the binding constraint there is C3--C4
rather than C2, while the remaining dense scenarios are schedule-limited
by C2. Splitting each dense class by this classification, the two
subpopulations carry distinct quality profiles: at S4 the C3--C4-bound
scenarios average $f_2 = 0.365$, $f_3 = 0.643$ (G-BL class means),

packing under the shared cap thins per-task quality --- while at S3 the
two groups are closer ($0.346$, $0.606$ vs.\ $0.339$, $0.623$). MOEA-3
reproduces the same split (S4: $0.366$/$0.647$ vs.\ $0.305$/$0.720$), so
class-mean quality margins partly reflect the bound/unbound mix within
each class. The A--D ablation invariance is, if anything, reinforced when
all variants are pinned to a shared resource cap (\S~\ref{sec:ablation}),
but dense-regime coverage convergence should be read as convergence to
the feasible task count under both constraint families rather than to
temporal saturation alone.

radiometric objective: at sparse density MOEA-3 separates visibly from G-BL
and G-SM sits in its own high-$f_3$ regime, whereas the dense panels show all
solvers converging to nearly identical distributions, consistent with
Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles}. Acquisition timeliness, now measured rather
than assumed: relative to the earliest window starts, G-BL itself drifts by
class-means of $100$--$652$\,s because greedy sequencing defers tasks
cumulatively, so the knee-vs-G-BL difference is the meaningful quantity---and it is
confined to the sparse regime: the quality-steering knee schedules lag G-BL
by $167$\,s on average at S1, while at S2--S4 the class-mean delays agree
within $4$\,s.

% ── 6.3 Squint-Aware Special Regime (Fig 2) ──
\subsection{Squint-Aware Special Regime}
\label{sec:squint-special}
The squint-minimized greedy solver (G-SM) improves NESZ radiometric quality ($f_3$)
relative to the profit-only baseline (G-BL) by selecting observation times that
minimize squint angle within each visibility window.
Across all 200 scenarios, this produces a large positive effect on $f_3$
(matched-pairs sign statistic $\delta_{\text{pairs}} = +0.99$, $p \approx 1.5\times10^{-34}$, on a per-scenario
paired basis: 199 of 200 scenarios improve) at an aggregate cost
in coverage ($\delta_{\text{pairs}} = -1.0$, $p < 10^{-34}$, on raw $f_1$; $f_1^*$ is not used here because G-BL yields constant $f_1^* = 1.0$ by definition, for which the normalized-profit comparison degenerates to a one-sample test against a fixed value).
The trade-off, however, is strongly density-dependent.
In sparse scenarios (S1, $N = 20$), G-SM drops to $f_1^* = 0.51 \pm 0.16$
($-49\%$ vs.\ G-BL at $1.00$) while improving $f_3$ from $0.497 \pm 0.044$ to
$0.722 \pm 0.058$ ($+45\%$).
In dense scenarios the $f_1$ cost remains severe: it peaks at S3
($N = 300$: $f_1^* = 0.29 \pm 0.04$, $-71\%$), eases slightly at S4
($N = 500$: $0.37 \pm 0.12$, $-63\%$), while $f_3$ rises from
$0.675 \pm 0.074$ to $0.786 \pm 0.046$ ($+16\%$ at S4).
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2} visualizes this density-dependent trade-off: the
Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS)
trend in panel (c) identifies a transition region around $N = 100$
where the $f_1$ penalty grows rapidly.
The radiometric advantage narrows with density ($+45\%$ at S1 to
$+16\%$ at S4) as tightly packed time windows constrain how far each
acquisition can be moved toward the squint-minimizing time, while the
coverage cost remains large at every density (G-SM schedules, on average,
$44\%$ of the tasks G-BL selects at S1, falling to $\sim$20--27\% at
$N \geq 100$; these are means of the per-scenario scheduled-task-count
ratios, the profit-side analogue of which appears in
Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles}).

% ── 6.4 Mechanism of Pareto Compression ──
\subsection{Mechanism of Pareto Compression}\label{sec:pareto-coupling}

The dense-regime convergence reported in
\S\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}, before the special regime of
\S\ref{sec:squint-special}, has a geometric
cause: the two physical objectives conflict most strongly when the scheduler
can move acquisitions, and coverage pressure takes that freedom away.

\paragraph{Compression mechanism.}\label{par:front-compression}
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4} shows a representative S4 front that contains
$4.7 \pm 4.4$ MOEA-3 non-dominated solutions (MOEA-2: $1.6 \pm 0.9$) yet spans
only about $0.08$ in $f_1^*$ and $0.014$ in $f_2$, against a total span of
$\Delta f_2 \approx 0.14$ in sparse S1 scenarios. The MOEA-2 and MOEA-3 fronts
nearly coincide at this density, so the third objective adds little
differentiation there. The compression characterises the G-BL-seeded front near
the full-coverage working point, not the feasible surface as a whole: the
squint-minimizing heuristic G-SM reaches $f_3 \approx 0.79$ at
$f_1^* \approx 0.37$ at S4, a region outside the MOEA-3 surface
(\S\ref{sec:squint-special}).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig4_pareto_front.pdf}
    \caption{Pareto front on a representative S4 scenario ($N=500$): MOEA-3
    frontier with MOEA-2 overlay and baseline solver markers. Panel (a):
    $f_1^*$--$f_2$; panel (b): $f_1^*$--$f_3$.}
    \label{fig:fig4}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Null hypothesis and coupling decomposition.}\label{par:null-decomposition}
Because $\theta$ and $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ both derive from the same LOS vector
$\mathbf{l} = (l_x, l_y, l_z)$ (\S~\ref{sec:system-model}), the per-task $f_2$
and $f_3$ contributions share geometric structure. We separate this
definitional coupling from scheduler-induced correlation with an
independent-angle null over the accessible envelope
($\theta_{\text{elev}} \in [16^\circ, 41^\circ]$,
$|\psi_{\text{sq}}| \leq 45^\circ$, the experimental squint envelope of
\S\ref{sec:constraints}; the satellite-side counterpart of the C1 incidence
range $[18^\circ, 47^\circ]$). A permutation null that shuffles the
visibility-window-grid $\theta_{\text{elev}}$ and $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ marginals
within each density class gives $r_{\text{null}} = -0.60$ to $-0.62$ across
classes (observed shuffle range within $\pm 0.02$ over 20 shuffles per class;
a nominal 95\% interval would require an order of magnitude more draws), and
Monte Carlo sampling over the
uniform box gives $r_{\text{MC}} = -0.28$ ($N_{\text{MC}} = 2\times 10^6$). We
adopt the empirical-marginal permutation value as the factorized null,
because the permutation preserves the empirical marginals and is therefore
the correct null for ``same marginals, no LOS coupling'', whereas the
uniform-box Monte Carlo assumes an idealized geometry:
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{null}} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(f_2, f_3)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(f_2)\,\mathrm{Var}(f_3)}} \;\approx\; -0.60,
\label{eq:rnull}
\end{equation}
where independence under the null factorizes the moments, e.g.\
$E[f_2 f_3] = E[\sin\theta_{\text{elev}}\cos^3\theta_{\text{elev}}]\,E[\cos^4\psi_{\text{sq}}]$.
The negative sign marks a structural conflict: in the
$\theta_{\text{elev}}$-only limit (fixed squint), $f_{2,i} \propto
\sin\theta_{\text{elev}}$ and $f_{3,i} \propto \cos^3\theta_{\text{elev}}$ are
strictly anti-monotonic ($r_\theta = -0.997$ in Monte Carlo), since $f_2$
favours high incidence and $f_3$ favours low incidence; in the
$\psi_{\text{sq}}$-only limit both rise with $\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$
($r_\psi = +0.997$). The wide-envelope Monte Carlo value ($-0.28$) is weaker
than the broadside limit because the positive $\psi$-common-mode dilutes the
$\theta_{\text{elev}}$ conflict.

\textbf{Correlation inside schedules.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig7} reports the pooled per-task correlation within
solver-selected schedules for the four objective variants. For every
variant the correlated pair is the same fixed full-physics $(f_2, f_3)$
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:f2} and~\ref{eq:f3}), evaluated on that variant's own
schedules; the variant-specific objectives steer task selection but are
never themselves the correlated quantities. For the full-physics
variant A it is $-0.62$ at S1 and $-0.15$, $-0.13$, $-0.23$ at S2--S4, with
per-schedule means of $-0.72$ and $-0.15$/$-0.16$/$-0.18$ (Fisher-z means
$-1.09$/$-0.16$/$-0.16$/$-0.19$ over the 50 schedules per class), and
individual S1 schedules span $r \in [-0.97, -0.03]$, all negative. Sparse
schedules therefore recover the independent-angle conflict of
Eq.~\ref{eq:rnull}, while dense schedules weaken below the envelope value. The
envelope itself, evaluated over all visibility-window time steps (20 scenarios,
five per class, 10\,s resolution; $7.6\times10^4$ geometry samples), gives
$\mathrm{corr}(f_2,f_3) \approx -0.36$ ($-0.35$ at S1, $-0.37$ at S2--S3):
real visibility geometry couples $\theta_{\text{elev}}$ and $\psi_{\text{sq}}$
through the LOS vector, and the resulting positive common mode dilutes the
$\theta_{\text{elev}}$ conflict. Every solver shifts acquisitions toward lower
squint than the envelope average (mean $|\psi_{\text{sq}}|$
$16$--$24^\circ$), and coverage packing weakens the correlation further at high
density.

\textbf{Which objective component steers the conflict.}
Variant B removes squint from the objectives ($f_2=\sin\theta_{\text{elev}}$,
$f_3=\cos^3\theta_{\text{elev}}$) and the sparse correlation collapses to
$-0.23$, close to the envelope value $-0.36$; variant C removes incidence
($f_2=\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$, $f_3=\cos^3\psi_{\text{sq}}$) and the correlation
holds at $-0.65$, indistinguishable from variant A; variant D removes the
physical objectives altogether and falls to $-0.13$ at S1 and $-0.07$ at S2.
Configurations that see $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ select schedules spanning the
$\theta_{\text{elev}}$ trade-off, whereas configurations blind to squint leave
the within-schedule geometry at envelope statistics. The mechanical
alternative, that dense correlations weaken because the realized $f_2$ range
contracts, does not hold: the pooled within-schedule standard deviation of
the \emph{per-task} $f_2$ component is $0.16$--$0.17$ at S1--S3 and $0.14$ at
S4 for variant A, with a pooled per-task range of $0.64$--$0.65$ across classes ---
a different object from the schedule-level $f_2$ objective whose pooled
bounds ($0.11$--$0.56$) enter the HV normalization of
\S~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}. The mechanism is alignment: reducing
$\psi_{\text{sq}}$ simultaneously improves both resolution components through
the same $\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$ factor (azimuth resolution enters $f_2$
directly, and $\cos\xi = \cos\theta_{\text{elev}}\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$
carries the same factor into the $R^3$ radiometric index), so the residual
two-axis structure $[\theta_{\text{elev}}, \psi_{\text{sq}}]$ carries its
genuine conflict on the $\theta_{\text{elev}}$ axis alone ($f_2$ grows with
$\sin\theta_{\text{elev}}$, $f_3$ with $\cos^3\theta_{\text{elev}}$ at fixed
squint, while $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ enters both objectives with the same sign).
In practice, the zero-squint point coincides with the time of closest
approach, which minimizes the slant range and, for a spherical Earth,
approximately minimizes the off-nadir angle $\xi$ (i.e.\ maximizes
$\cos\xi$ and hence $f_3$); solver convergence to low-squint regimes
removes the alignment axis and leaves the $\theta_{\text{elev}}$ trade-off,
whose exploitation is what saturates under coverage pressure at high density
(the factorized null of Eq.~\ref{eq:rnull} and its axis decomposition above
separate the definitional coupling from the scheduler-induced component).
All fronts
reported here are approximate, obtained by NSGA-III under the tested search
budget.

\paragraph{Value of the third objective.}\label{par:third-objective}
MOEA-3 separates from MOEA-2 in $f_3$ mainly at sparse density. At S1 it
attains $0.698\pm0.039$ against $0.657\pm0.040$ ($+6.2\%$ under the reported
knee rules, paired Wilcoxon $p = 5.5\times10^{-13}$; matched-pairs sign
statistic $+0.88$, 47 of 50 scenarios; conventional Cliff's $\delta = +0.57$)
at a small $f_2$ cost ($0.376\pm0.050$ vs.\ $0.393\pm0.055$, $-4\%$,
$p = 0.017$). Because each solver's knee maximises its own objective sum
(\S~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics}), we re-selected both fronts under a matched
radiometric-aware rule, which gives a controlled margin of $+4.9\%$
($p = 2.3\times10^{-11}$, 47 of 50), against $+0.05\%$ ($p = 0.86$) for a
radiometric-blind rule. The two rules apply the identical procedure (min--max
normalisation within each front, then the point maximising the normalised sum;
$f_3$ is evaluated post hoc on every point of both fronts) and differ only in
the radiometric term, so the $+4.9\%$ is the objective's own contribution and
the residual gap of the headline comparison comes from knee selection. The
margin survives a change of the reference-direction count: MOEA-3 at S1 with
Das--Dennis $p=4$ ($H=15$, comparable to MOEA-2's $H=13$) gives
$f_3 = 0.702\pm0.041$, statistically indistinguishable from the $p=12$ value
$0.698$ ($H=91$), so the gain does not come from the $7\times$ larger
reference-direction set. At S4 the class means coincide ($0.680\pm0.073$ for
both), although MOEA-3 keeps a consistently signed per-scenario edge (33 of 50,
sign statistic $+0.32$, $p = 4.5\times10^{-3}$; conventional Cliff's
$\delta = +0.03$, i.e.\ negligible in distribution). Under the elevation-plane
decomposition of \S\ref{sec:objective-functions}, MOEA-2 already attains high
$f_3$ post hoc, so the marginal value of optimising $f_3$ explicitly is
bounded; at operational densities coverage pressure locks the selection, while
the underlying $f_2$--$f_3$ trade-off persists, with a negative
within-schedule correlation at every density.

\paragraph{Hypervolume as a secondary diagnostic.}\label{par:hv-diagnostic}
Table~\ref{tab:hv} ranks G-BL first in coverage and last in HV, and the
NSGA-III variants first and second in HV despite their lower coverage. G-SM,
omitted from the table, attains the highest HV of any solver ($0.2341$) from
its low-coverage, high-$f_3$ operating point ($f_1^* \approx 0.37$,
$f_3 \approx 0.79$ at S4), so the ranking would invert if it were included. HV
rewards an extreme single point and is insensitive to cardinality: every
single-objective solver contributes one point per scenario, while MOEA-3
contributes $33.8 \pm 8.4$ non-dominated points at S1 (MOEA-2: $6.8 \pm 2.1$).
We therefore report HV as a secondary diagnostic only. Consistent with the
saturation above, the per-density hypervolume contracts at higher density:
at S4 the pooled HV is $0.25$ (MOEA-2) and $0.17$ (MOEA-3) of its S1 value,
reflecting the near-point front compression documented above and not a
degradation of solution quality. In one of the 200
scenarios a sparse-density MOEA solution exceeds $f_1^*=1.00$ ($+10.8\%$ above
G-BL), so the greedy schedule is a near-optimal coverage anchor
(\S~\ref{sec:overall-performance}). The front compression of
\S\ref{par:front-compression} does not depend on the HV ranking.

\paragraph{Non-optimality of the discarded set.}\label{par:non-optimality}
Because each quality objective is a mean over the scheduled set, and thus
selection-invariant, one must ask whether solvers raise it
by discarding poorly-geometried targets. They do not: the targets the solvers
drop have, if anything, \emph{higher} best-achievable radiometric quality than
the retained set (mean window-best $\cos^3\xi$: $0.92$ dropped vs.\ $0.80$
retained for MOEA-3 at S1, $0.95$ vs.\ $0.79$ for MOEA-2; gap $0.86$ vs.\
$0.82$ at S4). Dropping is not quality-ranked --- it prices coverage profit
and scheduling feasibility, not per-task quality --- so the quality gains
reported above come from timing selection within windows, not from
cherry-picking the target set.

\paragraph{Initialization.}
Random-initialized MOEA-2 loses $0.393 \pm 0.251$, $0.551 \pm 0.060$,
$0.684 \pm 0.050$, and $0.475 \pm 0.067$ in $f_1^*$ at S1--S4 relative to
hot-start MOEA-2 (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig8}a; 10/4/5/5 scenarios with three seeds
each). G-BL seeding therefore drives the coverage convergence. The effect is
not confined to coverage: random initialization attains higher $f_2$ at sparse
density ($0.505$ vs.\ $0.395$ at S1, $+28\%$; $0.460$ vs.\ $0.320$ at S2,
$+44\%$), marginally higher at S3 ($0.349$ vs.\ $0.339$, $+3\%$), and lower
only at the densest S4 ($0.347$ vs.\ $0.380$, $-9\%$). The G-BL anchor's
material benefit is coverage, and its geometric advantage is confined to the
densest regime. Because the headline experiments hot-start from G-BL, the
sparse-regime margins are anchored-front values, and a merged
hot-start-plus-random-init front could extend the quality--coverage envelope
beyond either run alone.

\paragraph{Perturbation.}
A sweep over $\sigma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7\}$ on MOEA-3 (10 S3 scenarios per
level, fixed solver seed) leaves quality flat: mean $f_2 \approx 0.343$ and
mean $f_3 \approx 0.610$ at every level, with level-to-level variation of at
most $\sim 1\times10^{-3}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig8}b; MOEA-2 behaves the same, at
$f_2 \approx 0.343$ and $f_3 \approx 0.609$), and coverage stays flat at 200 of the 300 targets (all ten scenarios at the
ceiling for $\sigma \le 0.5$, mean 199.5 at $\sigma=0.7$;
\S~\ref{sec:constraints}). The coupling reported in
\S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling} is a property of the schedule geometry, not of the
perturbation magnitude. All control runs use the same Das--Dennis partition
($p=12$, giving $H=91$ reference directions for three objectives and $H=13$
for two) as the headline experiments.

\paragraph{Chance.}
On five S1 scenarios, 5,000 random task selections per scenario produce mean
$f_1^*=0.527 \pm 0.003$, a 90th percentile of $0.930 \pm 0.008$, and a best
sample of $1.000$, the G-BL profit (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig8}c). Unconstrained
random selection never exceeded the greedy baseline on these scenarios, an
empirical bound on the tested batch: the sampler does not enforce C2--C4, so
high coverage arises only by chance.

\paragraph{Search budget and random initialization.}
Doubling the budget (population 200, 400 generations) on five S3 and five S4
scenarios keeps the A--D difference in $f_1^*$ negligible ($-0.12\%$ at S3,
$-0.44\%$ at S4; both variants reach full coverage), and shifts quality by at
most $+1.64\%$ relative to the standard budget. That shift is the same order
as the sub-$2\%$ margins it is meant to defend, so the control alone cannot
separate premature convergence from all runs reaching the same coverage
wall; the bound/unbound split of \S~\ref{sec:constraints} separates the two
subpopulations directly. A doubled-budget control on the five S1
scenarios---the sparse regime where the margins are largest---leaves the
A--D difference in place (variant A $f_1^*$: $0.931$ standard vs.\ $0.924$
doubled on the same scenarios; variant D $1.000$ in both), so the
sparse-regime gap is not an artifact of the standard budget. A random-init
run of the no-physics variant D (15 S3 and 15 S4 runs) reproduces the low
per-task squint of the hot-started runs ($f_2=0.349\pm0.016$ at S3 and
$0.347\pm0.015$ at S4, against $0.342\pm0.026$ and $0.336\pm0.039$) at a
coverage cost: it schedules $70.3$ and $117.8$ tasks at S3 and S4, against
$154.5$ and $142.7$ for the hot-started variant (50 scenarios per class;
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig8}d). The
lower-squint convergence is therefore not a hot-start residue; if anything the
seed biases hot-started runs toward the higher squint of the greedy schedule,
which inserts tasks at their earliest feasible time.

% ── 6.7 Agility-Envelope Sensitivity ──
\subsection{Agility-Envelope Sensitivity}\label{sec:envelope-sensitivity}
The agility-envelope sensitivity, previously an open qualification, is
tested directly: repeating the sparse $N=20$ configurations with the
window-generation squint cap reduced from $45^\circ$ to $25^\circ$ and
$15^\circ$ (50 paired scenarios per cap, identical targets and seeds)
shows that the multi-objective edge contracts monotonically with the
envelope. Relative to G-BL in the same scenarios, MOEA-3's $f_2$
advantage shrinks from $+28\%$ at the $45^\circ$ cap to $+9\%$ at
$25^\circ$ and $+2.3\%$ at $15^\circ$, while its coverage cost shrinks
from $15\%$ to $4\%$ to $\approx 0\%$ ($f_1^* = 0.85$, $0.96$, $1.01$; the
value above $1.00$ reflects the near-optimal rather than strict coverage
anchor, \S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling});
absolute geometry improves for every solver under the tighter cap
(G-BL $f_2$ rises from $0.295$ to $0.405$ to $0.448$), because window
filtering removes the low-quality high-squint options.

% ── 6.8 Coverage-Stratified Frontier Control ──
\subsection{Coverage-Stratified Frontier Control}\label{sec:frontier-control}
Beyond the coverage--quality exchange rate, MOEA-3's remaining value is
the coverage-stratified frontier itself, measured against the cheapest
alternative: a placement-only
interpolation family (a $q$-fraction of the greedy coverage schedule
re-timed by the squint-minimising rule,
$q \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$, $K=10$ random subsets per level,
feasibility restored by the same C2 repair the heuristic uses); that
family is feasible throughout, and at matched coverage (mixture coverage
at or above the knee point's) the knee point dominates the best feasible
mixture point in $185$ of the $200$ scenarios ($92.5\%$) for $f_2$ and
$184$ ($92.0\%$) for $f_3$.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary}
At
$N=20$ the three dimensions form a genuine trade-off
surface. G-BL retains $f_1^* = 1.00$ at a moderate $f_3$
($0.497$), MOEA-2 reaches the highest $f_2$ ($0.393$) alongside a
$f_3$ of $0.657$, and MOEA-3 trades $f_2$ ($-4\%$ vs.\ MOEA-2) for the highest $f_3$
among the NSGA-III configurations
($0.698$, $+6.2\%$ vs.\ MOEA-2 under the reported knee rules), so no solver dominates on more than one
dimension. At $N \geq 300$ all NSGA-III configurations converge to the G-BL anchor and
the tested multi-objective variants provide little additional benefit over
the greedy baseline.
Our experiment across 200 systematically varied scenarios yields five
interconnected findings:
\textbf{(1)}~the quality advantage of multi-objective optimization saturates with
target density ($+33.7\%$ in $f_2$ at $N=20$ falling to $+0.3\%$ at $N=500$);
\textbf{(2)}~the third objective is density-dependent rather than redundant:
per-task $f_2$--$f_3$ correlation within schedules stays negative at every
density, strongest at sparse density (\S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}), and
MOEA-3 separates from MOEA-2 in $f_3$ at sparse
density ($+6.2\%$ at $N=20$) with the advantage shrinking to $\lesssim 0.2\%$
at $N \geq 300$, confirming the coverage-pressure mechanism detailed in
\S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}.
\textbf{(3)}~G-SM occupies a distinct high-radiometric-quality regime at
density-dependent coverage cost;
\textbf{(4)}~solver outcomes become invariant to the choice of physical
objectives at $N \geq 300$ (ablation study); and
\textbf{(5)}~coverage convergence toward the G-BL anchor is conditional
on the hot start, not a structural property. The first four reflect
geometric properties of the two-orbit-horizon observation geometry
(finding (2) being an empirical convergence effect under those
properties, \S~\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}); the fifth reflects solver
initialization.

Taken together, these results establish that
the G-BL-seeded front we obtain near the full-coverage working point, while
offering multiple quality-coverage configurations,
is compressed by the geometric coupling above: the narrow $f_2$ range limits
practical differentiation between solutions.

The pooled-HV Friedman omnibus and pairwise contrasts are reported in
\S~\ref{sec:overall-performance}.

Independently of the multi-objective question, GA-P-BL is never worse than
G-BL by construction and coincides with it in all but one scenario
(\S\ref{sec:ga-p-bl}); the present claims therefore concern the marginal
value of adding physical objectives rather than profit-oriented search alone.

\subsection{Operational Implications}
\label{sec:operational-implications}

The scale dependence has direct consequences for mission planning.
In sparse regimes ($N \leq 100$), MOEA-2 achieves a $+33.7\%$ geometric-quality
improvement at S1 at a $14\%$ coverage cost ($f_1^* = 0.86$
at $N=20$ relative to G-BL). Whether this trade-off is acceptable
depends on the application: for maritime surveillance, the
knee-point gain at S1 corresponds to roughly a $25\%$ smaller
per-task geometric area factor (the ground pixel area of a task scales with
the reciprocal of the per-task index factor of Eq.~\ref{eq:f2}, evaluated at
the elevation-plane angle; equivalently, a linear ground sample distance
about $13\%$ finer at the knee-point geometry ($\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.87$);
the exact schedule-level area change additionally
depends on the selected task mix), and the coverage cost may be
justified; for area-monitoring missions, the greedy baseline remains
preferable. In dense regimes ($N \geq 300$), the multi-objective
advantage falls below $1\%$ improvement in $f_2$, while G-SM offers
the highest radiometric quality ($+16\%$ $f_3$ at S4) at a $63\%$
coverage cost; this gain comes from the one-line squint-minimization
heuristic rather than from multi-objective search. Read as an exchange
rate, this favors the quality-first heuristic when coverage can be
spent: G-SM matches or exceeds MOEA-3's radiometric quality at every
density ($f_3$ higher by $+3.4\%$, $+23.1\%$, $+21.3\%$, $+15.6\%$ at
S1--S4, from the class means of Table~\ref{tab:solver-profiles}) while
spending $49$--$71\%$ of the achievable coverage ($f_1^* = 0.51$,
$0.35$, $0.29$, $0.37$ against the G-BL anchor of $1.00$).

The operational choice is therefore application-side---coverage budget
versus achievable quality---rather than solver-side: the
coverage-stratified-frontier control (\S\ref{sec:frontier-control})
shows the knee point dominating the cheapest placement-only alternative.
The interpolation family (\S\ref{sec:frontier-control}) re-times G-BL's
selection only---it does not mix task selections, and its $q=1$ endpoint
is not G-SM itself---so the measured comparison bounds the re-timing
channel, not selection diversity.

The quality advantage of physics-aware multi-objective scheduling is therefore
specific to wide-envelope platforms (\S\ref{sec:envelope-sensitivity}); on
platforms with $|\psi_{\text{sq}}| \lesssim 25^\circ$ agility, or without a
dedicated large-squint timing/processing chain (\S~\ref{sec:constraints}),
the greedy baseline attains comparable quality at full coverage even in
sparse regimes. The logistic midpoint
$N_{50} \approx 51$ (\S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}) provides a coarse
indicator between the density regimes. Our results suggest a conditional
operational guideline: for sparse observation requests ($N \lesssim 100$)
on squint-agile platforms with the dedicated large-squint timing/processing
chain of \S\ref{sec:constraints} (the $45^\circ$ experimental envelope of
\S\ref{sec:constraints}), MOEA-2 may provide geometric quality gains
that outweigh the coverage penalty in specific contexts: the knee point
trades $14\%$ coverage for $+33.7\%$ $f_2$, while the best-coverage front
point still gives $+12.5\%$ $f_2$ at zero coverage cost, a configuration
preferable when coverage cannot be sacrificed; for sparse requests on
narrow-envelope platforms, and for dense monitoring campaigns
($N \gtrsim 300$) on the tested platform family, G-BL attains the coverage
reference, with the multi-objective variants adding little over it, while
G-SM attains the highest radiometric quality at a substantial coverage cost,
so the greedy choice follows the mission's priority. Indeed, the
MOEA-2 coverage gap relative to the coverage-only anchor is
within $0.4\%$ at $N \geq 300$ (Table~\ref{tab:scale-sensitivity}); since GA-P-BL
coincides with G-BL in all but one scenario, no meaningful separate gap
exists. The measured timeliness (\S\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}) adds a cost to
the quality gain only where that gain is on offer, and does not
differentiate the dense-regime choice.

\subsection{Transferability Across Remote-Sensing Domains}
The analytic device most likely to transfer beyond SAR is the
envelope--null--schedule decomposition of \S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}: separating
the geometric correlation over the full feasible envelope from the
within-schedule residual isolates where an objective's value is lost to
saturation rather than to a mathematical redundancy. The geometric-resolution
objective ($f_2$) carries directly to optical systems, where ground sample
distance grows with off-nadir angle (the cross-track optical ground sample
distance scales as $1/\cos^2\eta$, the standard flat-earth off-nadir scaling
for a fixed-IFOV pushbroom sensor; the pixel area additionally takes a
$1/\cos\eta$ along-track factor), and an analogous density-dependent
saturation is a testable expectation for such settings; the radiometric objective ($f_3$, NESZ) does not,
because optical radiometry depends on illumination and cloud cover rather
than on observation geometry alone. The decomposition, rather than any
single solver ranking, is the transferable contribution.

% ── 7.4 Limitations ──
\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
Our study is bounded by scope decisions that define directions for future work.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The density-dependent third-objective indistinguishability reported above is specific to the
single-satellite, two-orbit-horizon Stripmap setting: it reflects solver convergence to lower-squint
geometries that attenuate the envelope-level $f_2$--$f_3$ correlation while
the residual within-schedule $\theta$-conflict persists (negative at all
densities, strongest at sparse density; \S\ref{par:null-decomposition},
Eq.~\ref{eq:rnull}), not a mathematical
identity between $f_2$ and $f_3$. In multi-pass or multi-satellite
settings with greater angular diversity, the $\theta$-conflict re-emerges
and the third objective regains discriminating power; dynamic arrivals and
rolling-horizon re-optimization are out of scope here, and their effect on
the saturation threshold is untested.
ScanSAR, Spotlight, and TOPS modes introduce different NESZ and resolution
dependencies beyond Stripmap, and the scenario design uses a fixed orbit
(693 km circular LEO) without exploring sensitivity to orbital parameters.
The two-orbit scheduling horizon is likewise fixed: longer horizons grant
more scheduling freedom per target, so the density boundary should not be
read as horizon-independent (the single-revolution scenario variants are
used for robustness checks only, not for the boundary).
The quality objectives capture first-order geometric and radiometric
effects (ground-range resolution, azimuth broadening, $R^3$ NESZ scaling).
Throughout, $\psi_{\text{sq}}$ is the geometric line-of-sight squint, the
angle between the target line of sight and the zero-Doppler plane; this is
the quantity that enters $f_2$, $f_3$, and the C1 squint bound. Second-order
radar effects are treated as platform-level constants and are not modeled:
the geometric Doppler centroid $f_{\text{dc}} \approx (2v/\lambda)\sin\psi_{\text{sq}}$
reaches $10^2$\,kHz at the scheduled $16$--$24^\circ$ squints, one to two
orders of magnitude above typical Stripmap PRFs ($10^0$--$10^1$\,kHz), so
unmanaged echoes would alias outside the processing band and leave the
Doppler centroid ambiguous. Yaw steering cannot remove this offset: it
compensates only the small Earth-rotation component, whereas the geometric
squint is fixed by the target's direction relative to the velocity vector at
the scheduled $16$--$24^\circ$. A squinted acquisition therefore requires a
dedicated receive-window and PRF timing design, which is outside the model;
the projection loss itself is not absorbed by Doppler processing --- keeping
the beam on the target fixes $\psi_{\text{sq}}$, and the
$1/\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$ broadening in the azimuth resolution is what $f_2$
charges for. The echo-processing chain itself (receive window, duty
cycle, ambiguity control, PRF selection), the azimuth antenna gain roll-off
with squint, and squint-dependent azimuth processing are all outside the
model; the Doppler magnitude is used only as a scale, whose sign follows the
look direction and is out of scope. This idealization preserves the
relative ordering of acquisition geometries \emph{within} the simulated
platform family, which is what the solver comparisons act on; absolute
performance claims for platforms without a dedicated large-squint
timing/processing chain are out of scope. The sensitivity of the recommendations to the
agility envelope itself is tested directly in \S\ref{sec:envelope-sensitivity}.

Two further scope notes bound the operational reading: the C3--C4 resource
caps equate to a 200-task ceiling (\S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}) and bind in
roughly half of the dense S3--S4 scenarios, so dense-regime convergence
reflects both temporal (C2) and resource (C3--C4) saturation, and results
are conditional on the tested caps; the
dwell time is fixed at 30\,s, removing the dwell-versus-SNR trade-off from
the model. The utility model optimizes quality and quantity without an explicit
latency or urgency objective; acquisition timeliness is not optimized, but
the acquisition-delay statistic of \S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}
now measures it post hoc.

\paragraph{Threshold.}
The saturation threshold itself ($N_{50} \approx 51$,
\S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity}) is estimated within this synthetic, static
scenario family and should be read as family-specific rather than
platform-independent: real request streams are heavy-tailed and arrive
over time, and their effect on the threshold is untested (the priority
weights are drawn uniformly on $\{1,\dots,10\}$,
\S\ref{sec:experiment-design}). The threshold is likewise unchanged under the
bound/unbound split of \S~\ref{sec:scale-sensitivity} (cap-free subset refit:
midpoint identical, $n=253$; the 47 ceiling-bound scenarios show no
deficit).

\paragraph{Controls.}
The hot-start control experiment
(\S~\ref{sec:robustness-calibration}) indicates that G-BL
initialization materially improves MOEA convergence; the coverage
convergence reported here is conditional on this initialization, and
full-scale random-init experiments are needed to establish the unconditional
behavior. Similarly, the S1
coverage reversal under variant D (\S~\ref{sec:ablation}) now has a
partial mechanistic account: the diagnostic arm of \S~\ref{sec:ablation}
localizes the deficit to the quality content of $f_2$ (three objectives
without it reach full coverage), leaving the excess-above-anchor part of
the reversal to the crowding-distance account, which awaits verification
with alternative crowding operators.
The initialization and budget controls bound the findings above without fixing
population values: their samples are small (10/4/5/5 scenarios with three seeds
for the hot-start control; 15 runs per class for the random-init variant-D
control), the scenarios are the first seeds of each density class in manifest
order, and hot and random runs share scenario seeds, so every deficit is a
within-seed paired difference expressed on the G-BL-normalized $f_1^*$ scale,
on which the greedy anchor reads exactly $1.0$ by construction. The reference-point counts of MOEA-2
($H=13$) and MOEA-3 ($H=91$) differ by construction, but a matched control at $p=4$
($H=15$, \S\ref{par:third-objective}) confirms the sparse-regime $f_3$ gain is not driven by
reference-direction density.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Finally, we do not benchmark against learning-based
schedulers (e.g., \citet{Chen2025NeuralPriority}), which represent an alternative
methodological family; a systematic comparison of physics-aware
heuristic methods against data-driven approaches is left to
future work.

\section{Conclusion}
We formulated single-satellite agile SAR scheduling as a three-objective
problem in which coverage profit, geometric resolution, and NESZ radiometric
quality are optimised together with NSGA-III, and we measured where the
formulation pays for itself. Five configurations were compared on 200
scenarios across four density classes.
In sparse request sets, re-optimising observation times buys large quality
gains over the greedy coverage baseline at a stated coverage price: $+40\%$ in radiometric quality at a $15\%$
coverage cost, $+33.7\%$ in geometric quality at $14\%$, and a controlled
$+4.9\%$ third-objective margin under a matched radiometric-aware rule.
Two measured boundaries delimit the gain. It contracts monotonically as the
platform's squint envelope narrows, from $+28\%$ to $+2.3\%$ in geometric
quality as the cap tightens from $45^\circ$ to $15^\circ$. It vanishes at
operational densities: beyond $N \approx 150$ the fronts we find converge to the
greedy coverage anchor with quality margins below $2\%$. The vanished quality
margins themselves are an effect of coverage pressure, which removes the
freedom to move acquisitions, while solver convergence to lower-squint
geometry aligns $f_2$ and $f_3$ through the shared $\cos\psi_{\text{sq}}$
factor. The within-schedule trade-off persists at every
density (negative correlation, \S\ref{sec:pareto-coupling}), so the dense-regime
convergence is an empirical effect of the schedule geometry, not a redundancy
between objectives. Coverage convergence toward the greedy anchor is itself
conditional on the hot start (\S\ref{sec:robustness-calibration}), and GA-P-BL
coincides with the greedy reference in all but one scenario, which delimits the
benefit of adding physical objectives to profit-only search.
For mission planning the rule is direct: quality-aware multi-objective
scheduling pays on wide-envelope platforms with sparse request sets, and greedy
methods attain the tested coverage reference at dense cadences. The
squint-minimized heuristic matches or exceeds MOEA-3's radiometric quality at
every density, and a knee-point schedule dominates the best placement-only
mixture in $92.5\%$ of scenarios for $f_2$, so the mixed family's advantage is
an exchange-rate rather than a solver-ranking effect. The boundary
estimates and the coupling decomposition give a baseline against which
multi-satellite, multi-pass, and multi-mode extensions can be measured.
