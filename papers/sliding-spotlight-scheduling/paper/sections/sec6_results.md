# 6. Results and Analysis

All readings in this section are taken from the released artifacts at measurement conventions v5 (Section 5.2) and are cross-checked against the reading ledger that stores each of them with its unit, convention label and source path. The grid is the nine-point demand scan of Section 5.3, every point read at the registered 40/60 budget.

## 6.1 The coverage--demand curves

The result of the paper is the coverage--demand curve (Fig. 3): the served priority fraction of the best-covered member, median over ten seeds, as a function of the demand-tightening factor. Three families produce three different shapes, and the difference between the shapes is the result rather than a nuisance variation to be averaged away.

The 100-drawn / 99--100-admitted family is the one the tightening actually reaches. Its median coverage falls monotonically with the tightening: 0.4827 at $s=0.8$, 0.6884 at $s=0.9$, 0.9485 at the as-placed point $s=1.0$, then 0.9766 at $s=1.1$, 0.9812 at $s=1.2$, 0.9882 at $s=1.3$, 0.9882 at $s=1.4$, 0.9900 at $s=1.5$ and 0.9900 at $s=2.0$ (family R3H). Tightening the requirement from the as-placed point to the tightest point that still has a feasible individual costs about half of the served priority mass; loosening it from the as-placed point to the coarsest point buys back under five points. Most of the response therefore sits on the tight side of the as-placed point, which is the side an operator would actually consider. Table 2 decomposes how much of that tight-side response is the stripmap floor rather than the dwell mechanism. The floor-exclusion upper bound --- every non-stripmap task servable, each stripmap task servable only if its scaled requirement still reaches the 2.4669 m floor, priority-weighted and medianed over seeds --- already caps the family at 0.748 at $s=0.9$ and 0.501 at $s=0.8$: the floor accounts for 20.01 of the 26.01-point drop to $s=0.9$ and for 44.78 of the 46.58-point drop to $s=0.8$, and the residual gap between the bound and the observed medians (6.0 and 1.8 points) is the schedule falling short of a bound that is itself floor-driven. The three families covary in mode composition as they do in task count --- the control family is all stripmap, the 50-task family has no stripmap task, and the dense family is mixed --- which is why the same tightening reads as a floor step, a plateau, and a floor-dominated fall (Section 5.2).

The 50-drawn / 46--50-admitted family is nearly flat and sits high. Its median coverage reads 0.9764 at $s=0.8$, 0.9737 at $s=0.9$, 0.9764 at $s=1.0$, 0.9764 at $s=1.1$, 0.9960 at $s=1.2$, 0.9960 at $s=1.3$, 0.9924 at $s=1.4$, 0.9924 at $s=1.5$ and 1.0000 at $s=2.0$ (family R2F). On this family a tighter requirement is close to free in coverage: the two local declines, of 0.268 and 0.359 percentage points, are small enough that they are given their own accounting (Section 6.4) rather than read as a trend.

The control family is the all-stripmap one, 20 drawn and 20 admitted, and its response is a step rather than a curve. Its median coverage reads 0.5398 at $s=0.9$ and 1.0000 from $s=1.0$ all the way to $s=2.0$ (family R1C): the family either serves the whole priority mass at the stripmap floor or serves a strict subset of it, and there is no continuous intermediate regime for it to occupy. Its tightest grid point behaves differently again and is kept apart from the curve (Section 6.2).

Read together, the three curves say what the mode assignment decides. A family whose tasks are all stripmap has a floor and therefore a step; a family that mixes sliding spotlight and spotlight absorbs a tightening in its parameters and pays almost nothing in coverage; and the family that mixes all three modes, at the largest task count, is the one on which the tightening bites. That ordering follows the construction of Section 4.5 --- the modes and their parameter adaptation decide which tasks stay serviceable --- and it is not a property of the search.

## 6.2 The as-placed point, the floor step, and the cost of a tighter requirement

Every cost in this subsection is measured against the family's own saturation point --- the first grid point at which its median reaches the family's maximum --- and never against another family or another generation. That choice is what makes each number comparable to nothing but itself, and it is stated on every line that carries one.

Tightening is where the cost concentrates: from the as-placed point to $s=0.9$ the median coverage drops 26.0 percentage points for R3H and 46.0 for R1C, and to the tightest feasible point 46.6 for R3H, while R2F holds within 0.27 points (0.00 at $s=0.8$). R3H pays a coverage cost of 4.15 percentage points at the as-placed point against its own saturation at $s=1.5$, the first point at which its median reaches 0.9900. R2F pays a coverage cost of 2.36 percentage points at the as-placed point against its own saturation at $s=2.0$, where its median first reaches 1.0000. Both costs also have a paired per-seed form --- the median over seeds of each seed's own saturation-minus-as-placed difference --- which reads 3.98 points for the mixed family and 0.93 for the 50-task family; the paired statistic is smaller than the median-of-medians because several seeds already saturate at the loose end. Both as-placed costs are readings under the anchor construction at the registered budget --- no upper bound tighter than the trivial 1.0 is available at $s\ge 1.0$ (the floor-exclusion bound is trivially 1.000 there, Table 2), so they are not attributed between genuine C2/interval infeasibility and construction shortfall. A four-fold budget leaves the residual unmoved as well: at $s=0.9$ the mixed family's ten re-run units (160/240) keep the median exactly at 0.6884 with the search never exceeding the anchor, so the 6.0-point residual between the floor-exclusion bound and the observed median is a construction/C2 property at the registered margin policy, not a budget artefact. R1C's cost against its own saturation is 0.00 percentage points, and that zero is a statement about where the family's saturation sits --- the as-placed point is its saturation point --- not a claim that a tightening is free for every family. Own saturation is the grid argmax by construction, and its placement carries the grid: R1C saturates at the as-placed point itself, R2F's maximum sits on the grid boundary at s=2.0, and R3H's plateau is flat to within one priority unit from s=1.4 upwards. The premiums are read against those points, not against a grid-independent optimum.

The control family's tightest grid point is a different kind of fact and is separated from the curve on purpose. R1C at $s=0.8$ has no feasible individual in 10 of 10 seeds: every unit of that cell exited with the no-feasible-individual code, so the family admits no admissible schedule at that requirement under the frozen mode assignment. That is not a coverage of zero, and the two must never be interchanged. A zero would say that the search considered schedules and served none; what happened is that the requirement at $s=0.8$ asks for a resolution finer than the stripmap floor the family is fixed to, so there is no admissible schedule to consider. The figure draws the point as a cross against the axis carrying that label, the ledger stores it as a status rather than as a value, and no average, no smoothing and no line segment passes through it. Every statement about this point in the rest of the paper keeps the same distinction.

## 6.3 The frozen criteria and their verdicts

Table 1 reports the three criteria of Section 5.3 under the refined reading registered on 2026-10-23: an infeasible tight cell is undefined rather than a zero-coverage median, and the tight-arm verdict reads the median of the pairwise per-seed deltas (s=1.0 minus s=0.8) against a tolerance of one priority unit --- one count of the priority numerator, 1/sum_i p_i of the scenario, i.e. the smallest nonzero priority movement, which is about 0.86, 0.38 and 0.19 points for the three families at their registered task counts. The frozen verdicts remain on record, and the only cell where the two readings differ is the control family's tight arm: under the frozen zero-coverage scoring convention it read ``met''; under the refined reading it is undefined, because the tight cell admits no feasible individual in any seed. Why that cell is infeasible is arithmetic, not search: the control family is fixed to the stripmap mode, whose floor is 2.4669 m, and its coarse-tier requirements span [2.5, 3.0] m, so at s=0.8 every scaled requirement 0.8 x [2.5, 3.0] in [2.0, 2.4] m sits strictly finer than the floor --- no admissible mode exists for any task, and the infeasibility is an exact consequence of the frozen bandwidth and the frozen grid. The monotone verdict is met under both readings. The 50-task family fails S1 under both; its tight arm does not fall under either reading (the two dips of Section 6.4 sit below the one-priority-unit tolerance), and both failures are reported as results about this scenario set.

S3's failure carries information about the model, and it is the falsification of a reading rather than a null result. In the previous generation this family's tight arm carried a large penalty --- the median at the tightest point was 0.446 of the served priority mass --- and that penalty is *withdrawn*: under the convention-v5 model the same family reads 0.9764 at $s=0.8$, equal to its own as-placed value at the displayed precision --- not below it --- so the tight arm no longer falls at all. The withdrawn value is a historical comparison and is labelled as such wherever it appears; it is not a reading of this scenario set and it is not an estimate this paper stands behind.

What replaces it is smaller and better localized. The tightening still costs this family something, but the cost is measured at the as-placed point against the family's own saturation (2.36 percentage points, Section 6.2), not at the tight arm. The mechanism is the parameter adaptation of constraint C5: the mode parameters are derived from the requirement at the selected start, so the delivered resolution equals the requirement by construction, and a tighter requirement therefore spends dwell rather than serviceability. The family pays in imaging time; it does not pay by losing tasks, until the dwell term grows enough to collide with the transfer constraint. Saying that the cost has vanished would be wrong on both halves of that sentence: the tight-arm penalty is gone, and the as-placed cost is not.

S1's failure is the pair of local declines that Section 6.4 accounts for, and it is not removed by re-tuning. A criterion edited after its verdict is not a criterion: both failures stand as this scenario set's answer to questions that were fixed before its data were read.

## 6.4 The two local declines: an anchor-construction effect

Two local declines interrupt the flat band of the 50-task family: 0.268 percentage points between $s=0.8$ and $s=0.9$, and 0.359 percentage points between $s=1.3$ and $s=1.4$. Both are attribution results, and the attribution is not the one a reader would expect.

Neither is a search miss. Over all 180 sweep units of the two treatment families the best-covered member of a unit's tie-set equals the greedy anchor's value exactly, and the search-only maximum is empty; across the whole 260-unit sweep the search exceeds its anchor in the two control units of Section 4.5 and nowhere else. A decline produced by a search that never misses is not a search miss. It is produced by the anchor construction itself, which is deterministic and reproducible: the anchor re-times the all-selected schedule in a single bounded pass, and at a given requirement that pass can serve one task fewer or one task more than at the neighbouring requirement.

The per-seed accounting makes the mechanism visible. Between $s=0.8$ and $s=0.9$ two seeds move in opposite directions --- one loses 1.83 percentage points and one gains 0.42. Between $s=1.3$ and $s=1.4$ the same shape recurs with different seeds: one loses 1.14 points and one gains 0.42. In both cases the decline is the median of ten readings following two of its members, not a property of the demand axis.

This is a different object from the search-artifact verdict of the earlier line, and the difference matters for how the reading is used. That verdict retired a reading because a larger search budget removed it: what was retired was an artifact of the search. Here the anchor component of the reading is budget-independent by construction, and the search component was nil at the registered budget, and a four-fold budget increase (160/240, 38,400 evaluations against the registered 2,400) leaves it nil at both dip points of this family: over the twenty re-run units the search never exceeds its anchor (largest movement 0.00 points), so the dips are not waiting for a larger budget to disappear --- consistent with the registered single-budget decision (Section 7.4) and with the construction carrying the response. And nothing physical follows from either decline: two opposite single-seed displacements are not a monotonicity claim in either direction, and no statement about the demand axis is made from a movement in the third decimal place.

## 6.5 Demand satisfaction and retention

The curve says how much priority mass is served; two further readings say what that service amounts to. The served-task fraction (selected tasks over admitted tasks, median over seeds of the best-covered member) tracks the priority fraction closely on every family --- 0.507/0.714/0.935 for the mixed family at $s=0.8/0.9/1.0$ against priority fractions of 0.483/0.688/0.948, and 0.979--0.980 across the whole tight side for the 50-task family against 0.974--0.976 --- so the uniform random priorities are close to inert in practice: what the schedule drops under tightening is, to within about two points, a proportional slice of the task set rather than a priority-selected one (the small residual means dropped tasks carry slightly above-average priority). The all-stripmap control is deliberately omitted from this reading: its served fraction equals its priority fraction wherever it is feasible (both saturate together), and its step cell is arithmetic rather than a schedule outcome (Section 6.3). Over the nine demand points the tie set serves 477,308 task instances, distributed over 9,430 tie-set records: a served task instance is one tie-set member's selection of one task, not a distinct task --- the best-covered member of each unit serves 13,845 distinct tasks in total over the grid, a factor of 34.5 below the instance count --- and the count is the sum over every record of the tasks that record selects. The delivery identity that constraint C5 enforces is a per-mode rule: for sliding spotlight and spotlight the delivered resolution equals the requirement at the selected start by construction, and for stripmap the library floor is delivered at or coarser than the requirement. The identity is checked by a raise rather than by a score, so no artifact stores a per-record verdict; the observable is the constraint violation, whose maximum over every record is 0.0, and no violation is recorded relative to the evaluator for any unit of the sweep. Ten of the 270 launched units exited with no feasible individual (the control family at $s=0.8$) and are excluded from the served count by the driver ledger rather than folded into it as zeros.

The retention reading measures the same service from the schedule's side, and it travels with a convention that must travel with it. On the STR-clip-only convention --- retention is the fraction of the tie-set feasible measure that remains feasible after the stripmap clip (numerator: the tie-set measure still feasible after the clip, denominator: the tie-set measure before it), evaluated at the as-placed point --- retention reads 51.14% for the control family, 100.00% for the 50-task family and 71.00% for the 100-task family. The earlier line's retention figures, which were computed under the retired SLS/SPT narrowing, are superseded and are not quoted here.

## 6.6 The fixed-dwell counterfactual

How much does the endogenous dwell actually buy? The registered counterfactual answers by scheduling against the classical assumption. For each family and each seed at the as-placed requirement, a freeze-dwell arm plans the schedule with the dwell held at its zero-squint library value at every consumption point --- the same solver, the same budget, the same scenario population as the registered arms --- and the planned selection and start times are then replayed against the real, squint-dependent dwell.

The fixed-dwell plan does not survive the replay on the dense families. The transfer constraint is violated in 20--29% of consecutive pairs (median per-seed rates 20.2 and 28.9; aggregate counts 184 of 926 pairs for the 100-task family and 135 of 458 for the 50-task family over the ten seeds). The violation rate is measured before any repair runs and does not depend on what is done next; it is the robust, repair-independent reading of the counterfactual. What the violated plan then costs does depend on the repair rule, so three rules are reported, all evaluated on the same stored freeze-dwell plans (rules (ii) and (iii) are reanalysis additions of this revision, dated 2026-10-24, not preregistered arms): (i) the drop-later rule registered with the counterfactual --- drop the later task of each violating pair, priority-blind --- under which the replayed schedule lands 18.25 and 30.44 percentage points (family medians; per-seed ranges 11.99--25.56 and 24.54--35.18) below what the endogenous dwell delivers; (ii) a priority-aware drop --- drop whichever endpoint of the violating pair carries the lower priority, with backward re-check --- which moves the medians only to 17.06 and 30.11; and (iii) a bounded re-timing --- push the later task to the earliest feasible start inside its own windows, drop only if none exists --- which cuts the 50-task gap to 7.08 but *raises* the 100-task gap to 25.33, because on the dense family the pushed starts collide with later tasks and cascade further drops. The span 7--30 points is therefore a property of the repair capability, not a single number, and the two rules bracket it: the measured consequence of flying a plan that is infeasible against the real dwell is double-digit under every rule tested on the dense families, and which end of the span a deployment approaches depends on how much re-timing authority its repair has. The control family --- sparse, and all stripmap --- is essentially untouched: no violating pair in nine of ten seeds and a 0.0-point median gap under every rule (one seed carries a single violating pair), a reading taken relative to the evaluator as everywhere in this paper. That contrast is the signature of the mechanism: the fixed-dwell assumption fails exactly where the C2 chain is tight, and a planner working from a library-dwell model does not learn this from the plan itself --- the computed schedule looks feasible until it is replayed against the real dwell. The counterfactual is a reanalysis of the registered artifacts rather than a new experiment; the fixed-dwell ablation of the retired v4 generation (2026-10-17) is not mixed in.

## 6.7 Policy-knob sensitivities: transfer margins and tier placement

Two policy knobs sit under the curve --- the transfer-margin policy that prices every inter-task transition, and the demand-template tier placement that fixes where the stripmap floor bites --- and both admit reanalyses of the frozen artifacts.

**Transfer margins.** Re-running the greedy anchor (the construction face that carries the response, Section 4.5) over the same frozen mixed-family scenarios under four margin policies --- the registered one (25% dwell pad, 4.07 s fallback maneuver bound), the pad halved to 12.5%, the fallback bound replaced by the median measured model value (2.409 s), and both together --- moves the median coverage curve by at most 0.7 percentage points anywhere on the grid (the largest movement is 0.9766 to 0.9833 at $s=1.1$ under both relaxations) and leaves the floor-dominated cells $s=0.8$ and $s=0.9$ identical to the registered reading (0.4827 and 0.6884 under every policy: there the binding exclusion is the floor, not the margin). The registered-policy arm reproduces the stored sweep anchors exactly on all 90 replayed units, so the comparison is anchored. The margin policy therefore prices the absolute level by tenths of a point; it does not generate the tightening response.

**Tier placement.** Where the floor step sits is a property of the demand template, not of the platform, and the sweep can measure that directly: multiplying the whole coarse tier by $1/\phi$ is arithmetically identical to reading the same frozen manifests at demand factors $s/\phi$ (requirements, modes, windows and priorities all unchanged), so the all-stripmap control family admits an exact in-sweep tier-shift experiment. Under $\phi=1.1$ the predicted all-infeasible threshold moves to $s^{*}=2.4669/(3.0/1.1)=0.905$, and the observed step moves exactly there: the shifted $s=0.9$ cell ($s_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.818$) is infeasible in 10 of 10 seeds, and the shifted $s=1.0$ cell reads a median of 0.568 --- the same partial-band shape the original family shows at its own $s=0.9$ (0.540). Under $\phi=0.9$ the threshold leaves the grid ($s^{*}=0.740$) and no infeasible cell remains: the tightest cell reads a partial 0.469 and the next saturates. The step location is thus a direct function of the tier knob, and the platform's contribution is the floor value itself. The corresponding operational caveat is registered with the result: real specification changes move one product tier at a time rather than scaling the whole demand book uniformly, and per-tier tightening --- the axis an operator would actually use --- is future work; the uniform scan here is the controlled version of the question, not the operational one.

## 6.8 Reported readings

One reading is reported beside the curve. It is not an optimization target, and it travels with the convention label that defines the quantity it states.

**The fulfillment ratio.** For a served task the ratio is its f$_q$ at the realised window and the selected start's squint, over the best f$_q$ the task could reach over all of its windows at zero squint, so the ratio is at most one; it is averaged over all tie-set members and reported, never optimised. At the as-placed point the fulfillment ratio reads 0.6580 for R1C, 0.7764 for R2F and 0.7447 for R3H. Its label travels with every number it states: *[library convention $f_q$ | per-task $f_q$ at the realised window and squint over the zero-squint optimum over all windows | mean over all tie-set members | reported reading, not an optimization target]*. What the reading says is narrow and is kept narrow: it measures how much of the zero-squint optimum over a task's own windows survives the choice of window and start, not how good the image is by any calibrated radiometric standard (Section 7.3).
