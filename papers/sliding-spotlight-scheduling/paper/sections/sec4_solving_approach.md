# 4. Solving Approach

In scheduling-theory terms the problem of Section 3 is a single-machine scheduling problem with time windows and sequence-dependent setup times: the satellite is one resource, each task carries visibility windows, and the inter-task transfer of constraint C2 charges a maneuver time that depends on the attitude endpoints of the consecutive pair --- a sequence-dependent setup in the classical sense. The imaging dwell adds a second classical construct: because the dwell follows from the mode and the squint of the selected start, a task's processing time is not a constant but a function of where it is started within its window, a position- and time-dependent processing time of the kind studied in the time-dependent scheduling literature \cite{gawiejnowicz2009equivalent,gawiejnowicz2015structural,he2019time}. No complexity claim is made in this paper: no hardness proof or approximation result is attempted, and the evolutionary search of this section is offered as a measurement instrument on a registered scenario world, not as a contribution to scheduling theory. Bounding methods for the fixed-dwell agile-observation problem exist \cite{habet2010bounding}; how they transfer when the processing time is endogenous is an open question this paper does not address.

## 4.1 Encoding and evaluation

The solver is a single-objective evolutionary search over the chromosome of Section 3.4: a selection bit and a start fraction $\tau\in[0,1]$ per task --- no mode gene, since the mode is fixed by the product requirement --- so the densest real-window family (R3H, defined with the scenario families in Section 5.2) encodes the 99 admitted tasks (of 100 drawn) over 753 enumerated windows as $2\times 99=198$ decision variables; at the coarsest end the control family (R1C, 20 drawn and 20 admitted) encodes 40. Decoding projects $\tau$ onto the union of feasible window intervals of the admitted windows: a served task is feasible with respect to the generator's discrete 11-point admission rule (Section 5.1), and the evaluator performs no AASR recheck of its own --- feasibility statements in the results are relative to that generation-side caliber, not to an independent physical guarantee (Fig. 2).

## 4.2 Anchor-seeded initialization

The initial population contains the greedy anchor: a bounded single-pass re-timing repair of the all-selected schedule. The seed is what makes the $f_1$ normalisation meaningful --- every front carries a solution anchored at the served-priority reference --- and its injection into the population is checked by a dedicated cross-check that once exposed spurious violations from time-inverted index pairs, which is also why the evaluation loop sorts by start time before charging the C2 chain.

## 4.3 Search operators and selection

Variation applies simulated binary crossover ($\eta=15$, per-variable probability $0.9$) and polynomial mutation ($\eta=20$) uniformly over the real-valued chromosome --- the selection bits are ordinary real genes decoded by a $0.5$ threshold, so no separate binary operator acts on them. Because the problem carries one criterion, the non-dominated sorting of the standard machinery ranks by that single value alone \cite{deb2002a}, and the survivor set of a unit is a tie-set on coverage rather than a trade-off front: the search returns the best-covered selections it reaches and the tie-set records their common coverage (Section 3.3). The search runs under a fixed random seed per unit, single-threaded, with manifest task-list order everywhere (no set or dictionary iteration order may influence a run), so every unit is bit-reproducible.

## 4.4 The measure normalisation of $\tau$ and its implicit bias

One parameterisation choice is disclosed as intentional. A start fraction $\tau$ indexes position within the *feasible measure* of a task's window union, not within wall-clock time: decoding is the measure projection of Fig. 2. Because the projection is measure-normalised, a uniform grid on $\tau$ samples wider windows more finely --- the parameterisation carries an implicit weighting toward wider windows. This bias is registered as a deliberate design decision: it is not part of the main specification or its preregistration, and its registration is carried in the project's dated design-amendment record (review note of architecture decision AD-3). No selection mechanism in the loop undoes the weighting; it is part of the search design, and every reading in Section 6 inherits it.

## 4.5 The search face and the construction face

The response this paper reports has two faces, a search face and a construction face, and the separation between them is measured rather than assumed. Over the 260 units that produced an artifact (nine demand points × three families × ten seeds, less the ten exit-11 units of Section 6.2, where the search exits with code 11 when no feasible individual exists for the unit), the greedy anchor already attains the best coverage found for the unit in this study in 258; the search exceeds its own anchor in exactly two units --- the control family R1C at the as-placed point, seeds 100 and 105 --- by $+0.0427$ and $+0.0463$ of the served priority fraction. That is a statement about where the coverage response comes from, not a statement about solver quality. The chromosome can do only two things: move a start inside the feasible measure of its window union, and drop tasks from the selection. Which tasks remain serviceable at a given requirement is therefore decided before the search runs, by the mode assignment and by the parameter adaptation that carries the requirement into the dwell; the search's job is to reach the level that admission sets. Within this world the anchor is what reaches it, and the two units where the anchor does not are recorded rather than smoothed. The consequence for reading Section 6 is direct: the coverage differences between demand points are a property of the construction, and no statement in this paper attributes them to the optimiser.
